# 1. Device: present the bait, hide the hook

Brooks’s first device:

- present pleasure/profit/sweetness;
- conceal shame/wrath/loss/consequence.

Source text orientation:

- https://gracegems.org/Brooks/precious_remedies_against_satan5.htm

## Biblical logic

- Gen. 3:4–6;
- Prov. 9:17–18;
- Heb. 11:25;
- James 1:14–15.

## Modern application

Pornography/social sexual temptation can present immediately:

- visual pleasure;
- curiosity;
- belonging;
- status;
- secrecy thrill;
- romantic attention;
- escape from boredom.

What is not simultaneously visible:

- habituation;
- damaged trust;
- shame/concealment;
- exploitation;
- redistribution of images;
- sextortion;
- relationship consequences;
- spiritual hardening.

### Key line

> **The digital age did not invent the bait-and-hook structure of temptation; it can put the bait in the hand before the hook is even visible.**

Rhetorical/application line, not Brooks quotation.

# 2. Device: paint sin with virtue’s colors

Brooks identifies temptation by renaming/reframing evil attractively.

Modern examples can include:

- lust → “sexual confidence”;
- voyeurism → “curiosity”;
- obscene humor → “being funny/not uptight”;
- vanity → “self-expression”;
- manipulative flirtation → “just being social”;
- secrecy → “privacy”;
- cowardly conformity → “not judging people.”

### Guard

Each positive term can describe something genuinely good/neutral.

The device is **misnaming a concrete sin**, not condemning confidence, privacy or sociality as such.

---

# 3. Device: lessen/excuse sin

Brooks’s third device is extenuation/minimization.

Modern youth forms:

- “everyone does it”;
- “it was only a meme”;
- “I didn’t touch anybody”;
- “it was only online”;
- “it was private”;
- “I only watched; I didn’t send”;
- “it wasn’t technically porn.”

### Pastoral use

Not every defense proves settled hypocrisy.

But persistent minimization after clear admonition is a meaningful hardening indicator.

---

# 4. Device: show saints’ sins, hide their repentance

Brooks warns that Satan can display sins of godly people while hiding:

- their grief;
- repentance;
- humiliation;
- later holiness.

## Modern church-child form

> “Pastor X sinned too.”
> “David committed adultery.”
> “All Christian boys struggle with porn.”

The statements may contain truth but are used to neutralize urgency.

### Correct response

Do not hide biblical saints’ sins.

Also do not hide:

- Psalm 51;
- Nathan’s rebuke;
- consequences;
- David’s grief;
- calls to repentance.

### Strong line

> **Grace records the sins of saints honestly; presumption edits out their repentance.**

Rhetorical synthesis.

---

# 5. Device: present God as all mercy in a way that erases holiness

Brooks does not deny God’s mercy; he rejects using mercy as permission to sin.

Modern form:

> “God forgives.”
> “Jesus died for all this anyway.”
> “God knows I am young.”
> “We are under grace.”

without:

- repentance;
- fear of God;
- holiness;
- judgment;
- mortification.

### Project connection

This is exactly the danger of church culture that says:

> “He is a child/unbeliever; keep him in every role, maybe grace will eventually work.”

Grace preached without holiness can be functionally turned into reassurance of rebellion.

Biblical controls:

- Rom. 6:1–2;
- Titus 2:11–14;
- Jude 4.

---

# 6. Device: persuade that repentance will be easy later

This is one of Brooks’s most important devices for adolescents.

The temptation:

> sin now; repent afterward.

Brooks insists repentance is:

- beyond fallen human power apart from grace;
- a whole-person turning;
- not a trivial momentary act;
- not something to presume upon for a convenient future date.

Sources:

- original PDF / complete works;
- modern indexed text: https://www.eternallifeministries.org/tb_remedies.htm

### Modern adolescent version

> “I’m young.”
> “I’ll get serious after school/university.”
> “When I marry I’ll stop porn.”
> “When I’m baptized I’ll clean up.”
> “I’ll repent when I’m older.”

### Strong theological point

> **Planning future repentance is itself presumption on grace.**

Do not promise tomorrow’s repentance as if the sinner controls regeneration/softness of heart.

# 7. Device: make the soul bold around occasions of sin

Brooks’s seventh device is exceptionally relevant.

He mocks the inconsistency of praying not to enter temptation while voluntarily approaching its occasions.

Indexed source:

- https://sermonindex.net/books/writings-of-thomas-brooks/25

His remedies emphasize:

- avoid occasions;
- conquest ordinarily requires turning from occasions;
- do not play on the edge of the pit.

## Modern mapping

- unrestricted phone beside bed after repeated porn use;
- preserving hidden account;
- remaining in sexualized group chat;
- private meetings with a person who repeatedly draws into sin;
- following explicit-content accounts “just to see”;
- continuing app/platform whose primary personal use has become lust.

### Integration

Brooks + Owen + Matt. 5:29–30 provide a robust pre-modern/biblical foundation for radical digital boundaries.

No neuroscience is needed to justify:

> **flee the occasion that repeatedly feeds your known lust.**

# 8. Device: point to prosperity/rest of people living in sin

Modern youth form:

> “They do it and nothing happens.”
> “My friends watch porn and are fine.”
> “The sexually active popular kids are happier.”
> “Nobody got punished.”

This can turn apparent short-term impunity into moral evidence.

Biblical controls:

- Psalm 73;
- Eccl. 8:11;
- Psalm 50:21 — divine silence mistaken for divine similarity/approval.

### Particularly relevant line

Psalm 50 is important here because the wicked interprets God’s silence as approval.

Future article can connect:

> **“Nothing happened yet” is not a verdict from God.**

---

# 9. Device: emphasize the hardships of holiness

Brooks notes temptation by foregrounding:

- losses;
- crosses;
- reproach;
- suffering attached to holy walking.

Modern adolescent form:

- “I’ll be socially dead.”
- “They’ll think I’m weird.”
- “I’ll have no friends.”
- “Everyone will call me sheltered.”
- “I’ll lose the relationship.”
- “Purity means loneliness.”

This is one reason positive Christian peer/community formation matters.

### Biblical counterweight

- Matt. 7:13–14 does not deny the narrowness/cost;
- Mark 8:34–38;
- Heb. 11:24–26 shows Moses evaluating reproach against greater reward.

Do not sell Christianity as easier fleshly life.

Show a greater treasure.

---

# 10. Device: compare yourself with those worse

Modern adolescent rationalization:

> “At least I don’t send nudes.”
> “At least I’m not sleeping around.”
> “I only swear; they use drugs.”
> “I only watch; he shares it.”

The comparison changes the standard from God’s holiness to a worse peer.

### Biblical controls

- Luke 18:9–14;
- 2 Cor. 10:12 principle (context carefully);
- Rom. 3 universal guilt.

---

# 11. Device: corrupt judgment through errors that lead to looseness

Brooks includes dangerous error as a pathway to wicked living.

Modern article should be careful not to say every doctrinal disagreement is invented to excuse lust.

But observable sequence can occur:

`cherished behavior conflicts with doctrine → doctrine becomes increasingly intolerable → moral teaching is revised to protect behavior`.

### Guard

Causality can also run the other direction: genuine intellectual doubt can precede changed conduct.

Do not pretend to know motive without evidence.

---

# 12. Device: choose wicked company

Brooks’s twelfth device directly concerns companions.

Modern digital form amplifies company because:

- group is always present;
- private messages continue after school/church;
- geographical distance disappears;
- content can be collectively shared;
- peer approval is instant.

### Key synthesis

Baxter + Owen + Brooks converge strongly:

- corrupt company is ancient;
- personal weakness matters;
- occasions matter;
- peer mockery/pressure matter;
- modern digital technology increases continuity/opportunity.

---

# 13. Brooks provides theological interpretation, not a demon behind every notification

Critical guard.

Do **not** write:

> “The algorithm itself is literally Satan.”

Biblical framework:

- flesh supplies sinful desire;
- world supplies norms/values/opportunities;
- devil deceives/tempts/accuses;
- technology is a human-created environment/tool through which these can operate.

Avoid technology mysticism.

---

# 14. Parent application

Parents can use Brooks’s categories conversationally:

Instead of only asking:

> “Did you watch porn?”

ask:

- What was the bait?
- What consequence did you stop seeing?
- What name did you give the sin to make it easier?
- What did you tell yourself about future repentance?
- Which occasion did you insist on keeping?
- Whose approval mattered?
- Which friend/group made the sin look ordinary?
- What is the concrete “hook” you now see?

This is biblical soul diagnosis without reducing the child to a clinical score.

---

# 15. Church/youth application

A youth culture can unknowingly reinforce Brooks’s devices if it:

- minimizes “small” obscenity;
- jokes about sexual sin;
- treats God’s mercy as absence of consequences;
- protects popular corrupting members;
- makes holy children socially ridiculous;
- lets visible ministry continue despite known settled rebellion;
- assumes repentance can always be postponed.

### Strong line

> **A church does not need to preach false doctrine explicitly to train presumption; it can train presumption by making repentance perpetually postponable in practice.**

Pastoral inference.

---

# 16. Brooks and the “easy fruit vs narrow path” user theme

Brooks gives a much richer version than generic delay-discounting psychology.

Temptation works by selective vision:

- show sweetness;
- conceal hook;
- minimize guilt;
- magnify holy cost;
- promise easy future repentance;
- normalize through company;
- preserve occasions.

### Working synthesis

> **The broad way is persuasive not because the sinner has carefully compared two ethical systems and chosen the worse argument, but because temptation teaches him to look at the part of reality that favors desire and stop looking at the rest.**

This is theological/pastoral synthesis, not Brooks quotation.
