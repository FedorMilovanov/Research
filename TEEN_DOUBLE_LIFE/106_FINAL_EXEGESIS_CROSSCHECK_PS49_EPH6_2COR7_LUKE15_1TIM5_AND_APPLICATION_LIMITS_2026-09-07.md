# 0. Method

For each text distinguish:

1. **TEXT** — what the passage directly says in context;
2. **PRINCIPLE** — legitimate theological/pastoral implication;
3. **APPLICATION** — case-specific parent/church use;
4. **FORBIDDEN OVERREACH** — what the passage does not authorize.

This follows the already locked Research rule:

> `TEXT → PRINCIPLE → PRUDENCE/POLICY`

and prevents conservative application from masquerading as exegesis.

# 1. Psalm 49:16–21 Russian Synodal (= Psalm 50:16–21 MT/English)

## TEXT

God addresses the wicked person who:

- recites/declaratively handles God’s statutes;
- takes covenant language upon his lips;
- hates correction;
- casts God’s words behind him;
- participates/consents in concrete wickedness;
- sins with speech and deceit.

The larger Psalm contrasts acceptable worship with merely external/religious form severed from obedient relation to God.

Calvin’s commentary is especially strong here: he treats the target as people whose religion is external ceremony/profession while impurity of heart and life remains protected; he highlights the contradiction between lip-profession and hatred of correction.

## PRINCIPLE

Religious words and external worship cannot serve as moral cover for defended rebellion.

A person can say orthodox things while his conduct practically rejects the Word precisely where the Word restrains sinful affection and behavior.

## APPLICATION TO PART III

Safe:

> A church should not reassure itself that a known seriously rebellious child is spiritually fine merely because the child can sing Christian lyrics, recite verses, or perform a religious role.

Safe:

> Hatred/refusal of correction is a more serious diagnostic sign than mere possession of correct religious vocabulary.

## FORBIDDEN OVERREACH

- Psalm 49 is **not** an inspired children’s-choir eligibility statute.
- Do not say every child who sins and sings is the hypocrite described here.
- Do not equate one exposed failure with settled hatred of correction.
- Choir suspension remains a pastoral/ecclesial application based on role, seriousness, persistence, influence, safety and repentance.

## P0 NUMBERING GUARD

Russian publication reference = **Пс. 49:16–21**.

Do not publish `Пс. 50:16–21` for this text.

# 2. Ephesians 6:1–4

## TEXT

Paul directly addresses children:

- obey parents “in the Lord”;
- this duty is right;
- the fifth commandment is invoked as the moral background.

He directly addresses fathers:

- do not provoke children to wrath;
- bring them up in the Lord’s discipline/instruction/admonition.

Calvin notes that obedience is a major expression of the broader honor owed to parents and that parental commands are subordinate to obedience to God.

## PRINCIPLE

The New Testament does **not** treat children in Christian households as a morally neutral class waiting for regeneration before commands apply.

The same text that establishes real parent authority also limits that authority morally: parental seriousness does not authorize arbitrary provocation.

## APPLICATION TO PART II / III

Safe:

> “He is unregenerate, therefore no conduct can be required from him” is incompatible with Paul’s direct moral address to children.

Safe:

> Strong discipline/boundaries and the anti-provocation command must be held together, not played against each other.

## FORBIDDEN OVERREACH

### No modern age-of-majority rule in the text

Ephesians 6 does **not** define:

- eighteenth birthday;
- civil majority;
- independent residence;
- the exact point at which child-level obedience becomes adult honor.

Therefore do not write:

> “Ephesians 6 proves parents retain the same command authority over a 25-year-old independent child.”

Nor:

> “Ephesians 6 ends at exactly age 18.”

The changed-jurisdiction argument must be built from broader biblical household/family categories, not from an invented age line in Eph. 6.

### No parent omnipotence

“Bring them up” establishes duty/means; it does not promise the parent can regenerate the child.

# 3. 2 Corinthians 7:8–11

## TEXT

Paul is reflecting on the effect of a prior severe letter upon the Corinthian church.

He distinguishes:

- mere sorrow;
- sorrow “according to God” / godly sorrow;
- repentance toward salvation;
- worldly sorrow leading to death.

Verse 11 describes concrete earnest effects produced in the Corinthians by their godly sorrow.

Calvin’s commentary keeps the concrete church situation visible: Paul is explaining why he can rejoice over a sorrow he himself caused through corrective writing.

## PRINCIPLE

Pain, embarrassment, fear or tears are not identical to repentance.

Godly repentance is not sterile emotion; it bears a changed moral relation to the wrong and observable earnestness/turning.

## APPLICATION AFTER DISCOVERY

Safe:

> A child’s tears after pornography/sexting/double-life exposure may be sincere but are not by themselves sufficient proof that repentance has occurred.

Safe:

> Parents/pastors may look for fruit/direction over time rather than instantly restoring every privilege because the child is distressed.

## FORBIDDEN OVERREACH

### Do not build a seven-item salvation checklist from v.11

The sequence in verse 11 is not a clinical/infallible diagnostic inventory by which parents can read election.

Do not require a child to reproduce Paul’s exact Corinthian response vocabulary before recognizing any repentance.

### Do not deny real emotion

The text does not teach that tears or sorrow are suspicious by definition. The distinction is the **kind and outcome** of sorrow, not “emotion bad / stoicism good.”

### Do not equate relapse automatically with false repentance

This passage does not establish sinless performance as the proof of repentance.

Use together with broader perseverance/mortification/confession material.

# 4. Luke 15:1–32, especially 11–32

## TEXT — literary setting

Luke 15 begins because tax collectors/sinners draw near to hear Jesus and Pharisees/scribes complain that He receives sinners and eats with them.

The lost sheep, lost coin and two-son/father narrative answer that complaint.

The third parable portrays:

- arrogant departure;
- dissipation;
- misery;
- “coming to himself”;
- resolve to return;
- actual return;
- confession of sin/unworthiness;
- the father’s compassionate reception;
- the elder son’s resentment at mercy.

Calvin explicitly says the parable confirms God’s readiness to pardon returning/repenting sinners and exposes the malignity of those who murmur at that compassion.

He also gives a critical theological guard:

> the earthly father in the parable cannot renew the stubborn heart of his son; God alone changes hearts.

## PRINCIPLE

God is not reluctant to receive the truly returning sinner.

Repentance includes more than geographical movement: the narrative joins coming-to-oneself, return and confession.

Affliction/consequences can become providential means that awaken someone, but they do not possess regenerating power in themselves.

## APPLICATION TO ADULT-CHILD COMPANION

Safe:

> Do not use the parable to teach parents to make repentance difficult after it credibly appears.

Safe:

> A parent should be prepared for merciful reception rather than elder-brother resentment.

Safe:

> Material collapse may be one providential context in which a rebel begins to see his condition; parents need not erase every consequence they are not obligated to erase.

## FORBIDDEN OVERREACH

### Not a no-contact parenting manual

The narrative does not tell us:

- what conversations happened before departure;
- whether the father attempted persuasion;
- whether messages were exchanged;
- whether he received news;
- whether he “watched the road every day”;
- how long the absence lasted in modern calendar terms.

Therefore neither universal `CHASE` nor universal `NO CONTACT` can be extracted from narrative silence.

### Not an “engineer suffering” mandate

The father’s son suffers in the far country; this does not command parents to manufacture deprivation, danger or humiliation as a conversion technique.

### Not immediate restoration of every prudential trust/access category

The father’s robe/ring/feast is the parable’s image of lavish forgiving reception. It is not a direct statute about:

- passwords;
- vehicles;
- unsupervised access to younger siblings;
- ministry eligibility;
- financial stewardship;
- safeguarding restrictions.

Forgiveness and prudent access/trust questions remain distinct categories.

# 5. 1 Timothy 5:3–16, especially 5:4, 8, 16

## TEXT — immediate context

Paul is regulating care for widows and the responsibility of the church.

Where a widow has children/grandchildren, those family members are to:

- practice piety toward their own household;
- make return/recompense to parents/forebears;
- bear family responsibility rather than unnecessarily shifting the burden to the church.

Verse 8 intensifies the seriousness of failing to provide for one’s own household.

Calvin likewise reads v.4 as children/descendants repaying parental care and demonstrating piety first at home.

## PRINCIPLE

Adult family independence does **not** erase ongoing moral duties to parents/family.

The fifth-commandment pattern therefore has adult-life implications beyond childhood obedience.

## APPLICATION TO ADULT-CHILD LANE

Safe:

> “I am an adult, therefore I owe my parents no honor, gratitude, care or family duty whatsoever” is not a biblical model of adulthood.

Safe:

> Adult child and parent remain morally related even after child-level household authority has changed.

## FORBIDDEN OVERREACH

### This is not a text about continuing parent command-jurisdiction

1 Timothy 5 proves continuing **family duty**, especially care/provision in its context.

It does **not** say an adult independent son remains under every parental command as though still a minor.

### This is not an adult-child co-residence code

The passage does not directly answer:

- whether an adult son may live at home;
- rent amount;
- phone rules;
- curfew;
- eviction timelines;
- sexual-partner access to the home.

Those require broader moral principles + household stewardship + prudence + law/safeguarding.

# 6. Cross-text synthesis — what is now publication-safe

## GREEN — state strongly

1. Children are morally addressed by God now; unregenerate does not mean morally exempt.
2. Parents have real formative/disciplinary duties and are themselves morally restrained in how they exercise authority.
3. Exposure, distress and tears are not automatically repentance; repentance has moral direction/fruit.
4. God is eager to receive returning sinners; parents cannot manufacture regeneration.
5. Adulthood changes the form of parental jurisdiction without deleting family honor/duty.
6. Religious words/song cannot make defended rebellion spiritually harmless.

## YELLOW — state with explicit qualification

1. “Consequences may awaken” — providential possibility, not conversion mechanism.
2. “A rebellious child should lose a particular freedom” — application requiring demonstrated relation to sin and proportionality.
3. “Return home shows repentance” — only where confession/turning evidence supports it.
4. “Choir participation should stop” — depends on role definition, seriousness, influence, repentance and safety.

## RED — do not publish

1. `Пс. 50:16–21` as the Russian reference to “что ты проповедуешь уставы Мои”.
2. “Eph. 6 says parental obedience ends/continues at exactly 18.”
3. “2 Cor. 7:11 gives seven infallible tests of genuine conversion.”
4. “Luke 15 proves parents must cease all contact after an adult child leaves.”
5. “Luke 15 proves parents should cause suffering until the child repents.”
6. “1 Tim. 5 proves parents retain child-level command authority over independent adult children.”

# 7. Drafting consequence by article

## Part I

No major new correction from these five texts except Bible-quotation policy.

## Part II — Parents

Use Eph. 6 + 2 Cor. 7 as primary anchors, preserving the anti-tyranny and non-checklist guards.

## Part III — Church

Normalize central Psalm reference to **Пс. 49:16–21** and keep choir policy clearly inferential.

## Adult companion

Luke 15 and 1 Tim. 5 can carry substantial weight only if their different questions remain distinct:

- Luke 15 → repentance / divine mercy / return;
- 1 Tim. 5 → ongoing adult family duty;
- neither text independently supplies a complete post-18 parenting protocol.

---

# 8. Final verdict

**No core thesis requires withdrawal.**

The strongest improvement is stricter category discipline:

> **The Bible gives binding truths about holiness, repentance, family duty, mercy and authority; it does not give a one-size modern protocol for every phone, choir, adult co-residence or no-contact decision.**

That distinction makes the eventual articles more—not less—forceful, because direct biblical commands can be stated without contaminating them with prudential applications falsely presented as inspired rules.
