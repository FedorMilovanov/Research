# Deep Exegesis Pass — Hebrews 3 / Romans 1, 6, 8 / Matthew 5, 7 / 2 Corinthians 7 / 1 John 1

**Status:** RESEARCH CONTROL / NOT PUBLICATION  
**Date:** 2026-09-07  
**Method:** historical-grammatical first; Reformed/Puritan commentary subordinate to text; application to teen double life only after context is established.

---

## 0. Why this file exists

The project now deliberately rejects a psychology-first account of adolescent double life. These passages supply the primary biblical categories for:

- sinful desire;
- deceit and hardening;
- retained knowledge with practical rebellion;
- slavery/dominion of sin;
- Spirit-enabled mortification;
- radical removal of stumbling occasions;
- broad/narrow way;
- godly sorrow versus merely being exposed;
- walking in darkness versus honest confession.

No single passage is to be used as a diagnostic shortcut for one teenager. The aim is a controlled biblical synthesis.

---

# 1. Hebrews 3:7–19 — deceit, hardening, unbelief, daily exhortation

## Immediate context

Hebrews 3 contrasts Christ the Son with Moses and then quotes Psalm 95. The wilderness generation had received enormous covenant privileges and visible works of God, yet did not enter rest because of unbelief/disobedience.

The warning is addressed to the covenant community: “Take care, brothers...” (3:12). Verse 13 gives a communal means against hardening: exhort one another every day while it is called “Today.” Verse 19 identifies unbelief as the final explanatory category for the wilderness exclusion.

Calvin’s Hebrews commentary preserves the text’s sequence: evil/unbelieving heart → departing from living God → daily exhortation → deception of sin → hardening → perseverance evidence.

Source:
- https://ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom44/calcom44.xxi.iii.html

Owen explicitly treats Hebrews 3:12–13 as a warning that deceit in sin tends toward hardening, and insists that lust can make real progress in that direction even before “utter obduration.”

Source:
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/owen/mort.i.xiii.html

## Publication-safe claims

**Allowed:**

- sin can deceive before it openly dominates;
- repeated deceit can contribute to hardening;
- Christian community has an active duty of ongoing exhortation;
- external covenant/church privilege does not prove saving participation in Christ;
- perseverance matters as evidence of real participation in Christ.

**Do not overclaim:**

- “Every teen who hides pornography is Hebrews-3 apostate.”
- “Once hardening starts, repentance is impossible.”
- “Daily accountability mechanically prevents apostasy.”

## High-value application

For the project’s “he still knows it is wrong but no longer practically debates it” observation, Hebrews 3 is stronger than a generic psychology model. The key concept is not necessarily conscious redefinition of evil as good. It is **deceit producing hardness so that known truth has progressively less governing force**.

Working line:

> A person may retain the vocabulary of truth while becoming progressively less governable by that truth because sin has learned to deceive, excuse and harden.

# 2. Romans 1:18–32 — suppression, exchange, judicial giving-over, approval of evil

## Immediate argument

Romans 1:18 begins Paul’s indictment of human ungodliness/unrighteousness: people suppress truth in unrighteousness. The chapter does not portray morally innocent people who merely lack information. Knowledge of God is present at a real level, yet worship is exchanged, thinking becomes futile, hearts are darkened, and God judicially “gives them over” to desires/passions/a debased mind.

Calvin’s commentary preserves the repeated `God gave them up` structure in vv.24, 26, 28 and the culminating v.32: they know God’s judgment yet continue and approve those who practice such things.

Sources:
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom38.xxi.i.html
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/calvin/romans/1.htm

## Why this matters for the project

Romans 1 supplies a category more severe than “he came to believe this behavior is normal.” Verse 32 is especially important: **knowledge of judgment can coexist with continued practice and social approval of others doing the same**.

That maps better to some peer cultures:

`knows moral verdict`  
`+ continues conduct`  
`+ approves/rewards others`  
`+ becomes part of a social moral ecology opposed to God`.

This is not a claim that every vulgar peer group is the full Romans-1 endpoint. It is a biblical category showing that retained knowledge does not equal practical submission.

## Judicial hardening guard

The repeated “God gave them over” is divine judgment. Do not turn it into a casual parental diagnosis:

> “God has definitely handed this teenager over.”

Humans may identify alarming resemblance to biblical patterns; they cannot pronounce God’s hidden decree or final state.

# 3. Romans 6:12–23 — yielding, slavery, dominion, obedience from the heart

## Textual center

Romans 6 addresses those united to Christ. Paul does not say grace makes moral choices irrelevant. He commands believers not to let sin reign in the mortal body and not to present their members as instruments of unrighteousness.

Verses 16–19 use slavery/yielding language: the person becomes servant of the master to whom he presents himself in obedience. Calvin stresses that being under grace does not authorize sin; obedience is the practical antithesis to yielding oneself to sin.

Sources:
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom38.x.vi.html
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/calvin/romans/6.htm

Owen’s *Dominion of Sin and Grace* sharpens the moral responsibility side: people may voluntarily enslave themselves to sin, but sin thereby acquires no legitimate right against God’s law; the sinner remains in rebellion against his rightful Lord.

Source:
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/owen/sin_grace.i.vi.html

## Application

This gives a better biblical description than “bad habits took over my brain.”

Repeated sinful presentation is morally meaningful:

- eyes can be presented;
- hands can be presented;
- speech can be presented;
- devices can be used as instruments;
- social influence can be yielded to one master or another.

For a regenerate believer, Romans 6 also forbids fatalism: sin is not rightful lord and must not be granted dominion.

For an unregenerate teenager, Romans 6 cannot simply be imported as if he possesses union-with-Christ realities; but Owen’s broader moral point remains: slavery to sin does not cancel God’s ownership or duty.

# 4. Romans 8:12–14 — mortification “by the Spirit,” not self-salvation

## Context

Romans 8:12–13 says believers are debtors not to the flesh; if they live according to flesh they will die, but if **by the Spirit** they put to death deeds of the body, they will live. Verse 14 connects Spirit-leading with sonship.

Calvin reads the conclusion in antithetical terms: if the Spirit reigns, believers must not consent to flesh.

Source:
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom38.xii.iv.html

Owen makes Romans 8:13 foundational for mortification. He explicitly rejects both self-strength/self-invention and passivity: the Spirit acts in and with understanding, will, conscience and affections while mortification remains real human obedience.

Sources:
- https://ccel.org/ccel/owen/mort/mort.i.iv.html
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/owen/mort/mort.i.vi.html
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/owen/sin_grace.i.x.html

## Project guard

Do not reduce Christian victory to:

- willpower;
- dopamine reset;
- habit hacking;
- parental surveillance.

Those may describe secondary means or circumstances. Biblical mortification for the believer is Spirit-enabled warfare arising from union with Christ.

Also do not misuse this against an unregenerate child as though “try harder to mortify” were the gospel. Owen himself anticipates the question of unregenerate people striving against sin and rejects the conclusion that they should therefore “give their lusts their swing.” They remain obliged to resist sin while being directed to Christ as the only source of true mortification.

Source:
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/owen/mort/mort.i.x.html

# 5. Matthew 5:27–30 — lust of the heart and radical removal of stumbling occasions

## Immediate context

Jesus is correcting the reduction of the seventh commandment to external adultery. Matthew 5:28 moves the issue to lustful intent of the heart. Verses 29–30 then use right-eye/right-hand hyperbole in immediate relation to this sexual-lust context.

Calvin notes that Christ exposes the law’s authority over the affections, not merely outward civic behavior, and recognizes the eye/hand language as figurative/hyperbolic rather than a command to bodily mutilation.

Sources:
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/calvin/matthew/5.htm
- https://www.biblehub.com/commentaries/matthew/5-29.htm

The Greek `skandalizei` carries the sense of causing to stumble/ensnaring.

Source:
- https://biblehub.com/interlinear/matthew/5-30.htm

## What the text permits us to say

- heart lust is itself morally serious;
- one must deal ruthlessly with what repeatedly functions as an occasion/stumbling avenue into sin;
- loss of something genuinely useful may be preferable to retaining a pathway that repeatedly feeds grave sin;
- eternal stakes justify radicality.

## What the text does not permit

- literal self-injury;
- parental physical abuse;
- declaring every smartphone intrinsically sinful;
- assuming one identical “amputation” fits every child.

## Child-specific application

Owen’s temptation framework strengthens the prudential application: know the particular heart, natural frame, peculiar lust and occasions suited to it, then keep distance from the matching occasions.

Source:
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/owen/temptation.i.x.html

Thus a device/app/company boundary can be radical **because the risk is particular**, not because all technology is evil.

# 6. Matthew 7:13–14 — narrow gate / broad way

## Context and meaning

Matthew 7 concludes the Sermon on the Mount with a series of decisive contrasts: two ways, false prophets, false professors, two foundations. The gate/way contrast is therefore kingdom/disciple/eschatological language, not merely a generic self-control metaphor.

Calvin says Christ’s doctrine is opposed to fleshly licentiousness and that the broad road attracts because people flatter themselves and follow the crowd, while the narrow way leads to life.

Sources:
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/calvin/matthew/7.htm
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/icc/matthew/7.htm

## Guard

Do not write:

> “The narrow way means delayed gratification and executive function.”

That is reductionistic.

Psychological language about immediate reward may illustrate one ordinary human difficulty, but the passage demands an entire kingdom allegiance whose destination is life or destruction.

## Strong project line

> The broad way is not merely easier because teenagers have immature self-control; it is attractive because fallen people prefer a way spacious enough to carry their lusts without submitting them to Christ.

# 7. 2 Corinthians 7:8–11 — godly sorrow versus worldly sorrow

## Context

Paul reflects on the painful effect of his previous letter. His joy is not simply that the Corinthians felt bad, but that their grief produced repentance. Verse 11 gives observable effects: earnestness, eagerness to clear the matter, indignation, fear, longing, zeal, readiness to see justice done.

Calvin emphasizes the distinction between sorrow itself and sorrow that actually turns toward repentance.

Source:
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom40.xiii.iii.html

Watson’s *Doctrine of Repentance* is especially useful as subordinate pastoral synthesis. He distinguishes sorrow for sin from sorrow merely because one was caught or faces punishment; true repentance includes sight, sorrow, confession, shame, hatred and turning.

Primary sources:
- https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A65293.0001.001/1%3A7.2?rgn=div2&view=fulltext
- https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A65293.0001.001/1%3A7.6?rgn=div2%3Bview%3Dfulltext

## Application after discovery

The project should never equate:

- tears;
- panic;
- loss of phone;
- embarrassment;
- fear parents will tell pastor;
- temporary compliance

with repentance.

But neither should it demand emotional theater. Watson himself distinguishes rational/spiritual sorrow from the amount of visible tears.

Publication-safe pastoral markers:

`truthfulness`  
`+ hatred of sin rather than merely consequences`  
`+ turning/forsaking`  
`+ willingness to remove occasions`  
`+ restitution where appropriate`  
`+ continued openness after immediate crisis`.

These are pastoral signs, not an infallible heart-test.

# 8. 1 John 1:5–10 — walking in darkness, walking in light, confession

## Context

John’s concern is fellowship with God who is light. The false claim is not “I sinned once” but professing fellowship while **walking in darkness**. At the same time, vv.8–10 destroy perfectionism: those who claim sinlessness deceive themselves; Christians confess sins and depend on Christ’s cleansing.

Calvin explicitly treats walking in light as evidence/effect of union with God, not the meritorious cause of that union.

Sources:
- https://ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom45/calcom45.ix.ii.i.html
- https://www.ccel.org/study/1_John_1%3A7-9

## Crucial distinction for the project

1 John 1 does **not** permit:

> “A Christian never seriously sins.”

It does permit a contrast between:

- a life fundamentally brought into light, where sin is confessed and cleansing is sought;
- a profession of fellowship paired with an ongoing walk in darkness.

This is directly relevant to double life without pretending that one detected episode tells us the final state of the soul.

## Strong line

> The biblical opposite of hidden darkness is not a flawless biography. It is a life brought into the light: sin named, confessed and cleansed rather than protected as a second kingdom.

# 9. Integrated biblical model

The texts together support this sequence as a **pastoral synthesis**, not a universal eight-stage law:

`desire entices (Jas 1)`  
`→ occasions are tolerated instead of cut off (Matt 5)`  
`→ person yields members to sin (Rom 6)`  
`→ deceit weakens governing force of truth (Heb 3)`  
`→ repeated practice can become darkness/slavery`  
`→ known moral verdict may coexist with continued practice and social approval (Rom 1)`  
`→ mere exposure/sorrow is not repentance (2 Cor 7)`  
`→ true response brings sin into light/confession (1 John 1)`  
`→ for believers mortification is by the Spirit (Rom 8)`.

This model is stronger than the discarded pseudo-neuroscience line “brain turned off.” It is also stronger than the overly cognitive line “he decided it is normal.” Scripture allows a more sobering account: the person may still know, yet yield; still remember, yet suppress; still sing, yet walk in darkness; still feel some alarm, yet protect the lust.

# 10. Publication red-lines from this exegesis pass

Never say without qualification:

1. “Any hidden sin proves the teen was never regenerate.”
2. “A true believer cannot repeatedly fall.”
3. “If he cries after discovery, he repented.”
4. “If he relapses, prior repentance was necessarily fake.”
5. “Romans 1 proves God has finally abandoned this specific teen.”
6. “Matthew 5 commands literal self-harm.”
7. “Matthew 7 is simply a psychology of delayed reward.”
8. “1 John 1 teaches sinless perfection.”
9. “Church attendance proves fellowship with God.”
10. “Knowing something is wrong means the person is actively debating it every time.”
