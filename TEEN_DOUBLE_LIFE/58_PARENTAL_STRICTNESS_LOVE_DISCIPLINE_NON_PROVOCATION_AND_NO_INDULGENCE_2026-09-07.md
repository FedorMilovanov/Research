# Parental Strictness, Love, Discipline, Non-Provocation and No Indulgence

**Status:** RESEARCH / NOT PUBLICATION  
**Date:** 2026-09-07  
**Question:** How can Christian parents be genuinely strict about pornography, profanity, sexual sin, lying, corrupt company and digital access without either indulging sin or provoking children through arbitrary/angry control?

---

## 1. The false choice must be rejected

This project must not accept either equation:

`love = few boundaries`

or

`holiness = maximal severity`.

Biblical parental government has both a **positive disciplinary command** and a **negative prohibition against provoking/exasperating children**.

The point is not to split the difference mathematically. The point is that both are duties before God.

---

# 2. Ephesians 6:1–4 — children really owe obedience; fathers really owe Lord-shaped training

## Children are addressed as moral hearers

Paul commands children to obey their parents “in the Lord” and grounds the command in what is right and in the Decalogue’s fifth commandment.

This is decisive against a moral waiting-room theory:

> “He is not regenerate yet, so Christian moral claims do not meaningfully apply to him.”

Paul does not wait for proof of regeneration before commanding the children present in the Christian household/community.

Calvin notes that obedience is the evidence of the honor children owe parents and that human nature resists subjection.

Source:
- https://biblehub.com/library/calvin/commentary_on_galatians_and_ephesians/ephesians_6_1-4.htm

## Fathers receive a two-sided command

Ephesians 6:4:

- **negative:** do not provoke children to wrath;
- **positive:** bring them up in the discipline/training and admonition/instruction of the Lord.

This excludes both neglect and authoritarian volatility.

Calvin’s summary is especially useful: parental conduct should be mild/considerate **and** guide in fear of the Lord, correcting when children go astray; youth requires frequent admonition and restraint.

Source:
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/calvin/ephesians/6.htm

Exegetical reference sources note that `paideia` includes formative training/discipline and `nouthesia` warning/admonition. The command is not “keep them happy”; it is Christian formation that must not be carried out through arbitrary exasperation.

Source:
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/ephesians/6-4.htm

---

# 3. Colossians 3:21 — the danger of discouragement

Colossians adds the consequence-side warning: fathers must not provoke/exasperate children lest they become discouraged/disheartened.

Project implication:

A parent can be correct about the child’s sin and still sin in the **mode** of correction:

- unpredictable rules;
- humiliation;
- public shaming;
- insults about the person rather than naming conduct;
- impossible standards never acknowledged as met;
- explosive discipline driven by wounded pride;
- constant suspicion where every neutral action is treated as deception;
- using affection as leverage.

The prohibition on provocation does not nullify correction. It governs correction.

---

# 4. Hebrews 12:5–11 — discipline is ordered toward holiness and fruit

Hebrews’ primary subject is **God’s fatherly discipline of His sons**, not a parenting manual. The human-father analogy is subordinate.

Still, the analogy assumes that real fatherhood includes correction. Divine discipline has purpose:

- our good;
- participation in holiness;
- later peaceful fruit of righteousness.

Calvin explicitly contrasts fallible earthly discipline with God’s perfectly ordered discipline, whose end is the child’s profit and holiness.

Sources:
- https://ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom44/calcom44.xviii.ii.html
- https://ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom44/calcom44.xviii.iii.html

## Guard

Do not reason:

> “God chastens sons, therefore whatever harsh punishment a Christian parent chooses is godly.”

The text actually intensifies the demand that discipline have a righteous, beneficial end rather than being retaliation.

# 5. Baxter gives an unusually precise pastoral balance

Richard Baxter’s *Christian Directory* is highly relevant to the project.

He warns that terror and emotional strangeness can make children:

- loathe parental government;
- seek other company because home contains little relational delight;
- watch for escape/liberty;
- learn to lie when lying seems necessary for escape.

Yet Baxter does **not** answer by removing severity. His positive formula is close to exactly what this project needs: strong parental love allows real severity toward actual fault because the child can distinguish displeasure with the sin from rejection of the person.

Source:
- https://classic-literature.net/richard-baxter-1615-1691/a-christian-directory-part-2-christian-economics/

Baxter simultaneously commands parents to watch children’s tongues against lying, railing, ribald talk and taking God’s name in vain, and to keep them from corrupt company where possible.

Sources:
- https://classic-literature.net/richard-baxter-1615-1691/a-christian-directory-part-2-christian-economics/
- https://reformed.org/christian-family/the-duties-of-parents-for-their-children-by-richard-baxter/

## Baxter synthesis

The right contrast is not:

`strict parent` vs `loving parent`.

It is:

`loving + morally serious + predictable + proportionate government`

versus

`indulgent neglect`

and

`angry/arbitrary domination`.

# 6. Application to pornography/digital access

A parent may conclude that a child currently cannot safely carry:

- private nighttime smartphone access;
- unrestricted browser;
- disappearing-message apps;
- unsupervised social-media DMs;
- a particular friendship;
- a private gaming/chat environment;
- access to an account repeatedly used for pornography.

The parent need not apologize for a **strong boundary** simply because the child dislikes it.

However the parent should be able to answer:

1. What known behavior/risk is this boundary responding to?
2. Is it proportionate?
3. Is it understandable rather than arbitrary?
4. Does the child know what restoration path exists?
5. Is the parent acting from disciplined concern or rage/embarrassment?
6. Is the restriction protecting others too?
7. Does it preserve legitimate means of school, work, family contact where feasible?

---

# 7. “Punishment can create curiosity” — where this fits

The project previously gathered empirical material on reactance/psychological control. That evidence may remain as a **secondary caution**, but it must no longer govern the theology.

Biblically:

- Eve’s problem was not that God’s prohibition was psychologically unwise;
- a sinful heart can desire what is forbidden;
- therefore parental fear that “a rule might increase curiosity” cannot become a reason to refuse necessary boundaries.

At the same time, Ephesians 6:4 and Baxter forbid parents from making unnecessary provocation a parenting philosophy.

Preferred formulation:

> A wise parent does not remove a necessary fence merely because fallen hearts may resent fences. He also does not build random fences simply to prove that he has authority.

---

# 8. Child-specific protection is not favoritism

Owen’s *Of Temptation* gives a strong pastoral rationale for individualized boundaries. He says a person must know:

- his natural frame;
- peculiar lusts;
- particular weaknesses;
- occasions especially suited to those corruptions.

Source:
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/owen/temptation.i.x.html

This means two siblings may legitimately receive different levels of digital freedom because their demonstrated patterns differ.

Examples:

- one child uses YouTube openly and leaves devices voluntarily;
- another repeatedly bypasses filters at night;
- one friend group strengthens sobriety and church life;
- another group exists around sexual jokes, lying and hidden media exchange.

Equality of parental love does not require equality of exposure to every risk.

---

# 9. No-indulgence principle

Parents can sin by excessive severity, but they can also sin by **refusing to govern** because correction is uncomfortable.

Indulgence can take forms such as:

- “boys will be boys” about pornography/profanity;
- ignoring sexualized chat because confrontation might damage rapport;
- repeatedly restoring unrestricted devices without evidence of readiness;
- refusing to confront known lies because “at least he still comes to church”;
- putting a visibly rebellious child on church platform because removal would feel embarrassing;
- leaving younger siblings exposed to an older child’s corrupting behavior.

Love is not proved by making consequences disappear.

---

# 10. A practical parent sentence model

The future article may use language like:

> “I love you, and that does not change because you told the truth. What you did is serious. Because it is serious, some freedoms change now. I will not pretend restriction can regenerate you, but I also will not hand you an easy path back to the same sin. We will keep talking, pray, use the Word, seek help, and there will be a clear path by which trust can be rebuilt.”

This combines:

- relationship;
- moral clarity;
- consequences;
- no false salvation-by-control;
- no moral surrender.

---

# 11. Publication red-lines

Do not say:

- “If a child is angry at a restriction, the restriction was psychologically harmful.”
- “Strict rules produce rebellion.”
- “A loving parent should never inspect a device.”
- “Because a parent has authority, any surveillance or punishment is legitimate.”
- “External restraint equals sanctification.”
- “Because external restraint cannot regenerate, parents should stop restraining sin.”

Use instead:

> **Restraint is not regeneration; nevertheless parents are commanded to train, warn and correct. The same apostolic command that gives fathers authority also forbids them to exercise it in a way that needlessly exasperates and discourages their children.**
