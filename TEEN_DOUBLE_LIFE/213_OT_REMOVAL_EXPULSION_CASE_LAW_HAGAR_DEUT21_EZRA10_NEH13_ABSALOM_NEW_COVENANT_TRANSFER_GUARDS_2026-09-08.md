## 0. Executive verdict

The Old Testament gives **real categories of removal and separation**, but almost none can be transferred as a direct modern command:

> `person sinned seriously → Christian parent must evict adult child`.

The strongest legitimate transfer is more careful:

- God takes persistent rebellion seriously;
- household/covenant communities are not required to pretend severe corruption is harmless;
- protective separation can be lawful;
- repeated warning can precede decisive change;
- parents do not possess Israel's civil sword, priestly purity jurisdiction, covenant-land authority, or divine omniscience;
- modern co-residence decisions must therefore be argued from **current household stewardship, protection, non-enabling, fifth-commandment order, civil law, and New Covenant ethics**, not from copied Mosaic penalties.

Locked formula:

> **OT removal texts may sharpen the seriousness of holiness and jurisdiction. They do not supply a plug-and-play Christian eviction code.**

# 1. Genesis 21:8–21 — Hagar and Ishmael: a unique covenant/inheritance separation

## 1.1. What happens

Sarah sees Ishmael mocking/persecuting Isaac and demands that Hagar and her son be sent away so that Ishmael will not share Isaac's inheritance.

Abraham is deeply grieved because Ishmael is his son.

God explicitly tells Abraham to listen in this particular matter because the covenant seed will be reckoned through Isaac, while also promising to make a nation of Ishmael.

## 1.2. Actor / jurisdiction

- household patriarch Abraham acts;
- but he acts after **specific divine revelation** settles the covenant/inheritance question;
- the narrative concerns the Abrahamic promise and identity of the covenant seed.

Gill emphasizes precisely this: Sarah's demand cannot be treated as a free-standing rule because divine revelation concerning Isaac governs the event.

Source:
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/gill/genesis/21.htm

## 1.3. NT inspired interpretation

Galatians 4:21–31 explicitly interprets Hagar/Sarah and the two sons typologically in relation to the two covenants / slavery and promise.

Calvin therefore treats Ishmael's casting out principally within Paul's ecclesial/covenantal typology, not as an ordinary manual of domestic discipline.

Source:
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom41.iii.vi.v.html

### P0

Do **not** publish:

> “Abraham expelled Ishmael, therefore a Christian father should expel his sexually rebellious adult child.”

That erases the inspired Galatians 4 interpretation and the unique covenant-seed revelation.

### What may transfer analogically

- protecting another child/household member from serious hostility matters;
- family affection does not make every form of continued co-residence obligatory;
- decisive separation can coexist with real paternal grief and continuing providential concern;
- God may care for a person outside the parental household.

### But

`HAGAR/ISHMAEL = TYPOLOGICAL/REVELATORY UNIQUE CASE`, not modern eviction precedent.

# 2. Deuteronomy 21:18–21 — stubborn and rebellious son: seriousness AND limitation of parental power

This text is often invoked because it explicitly concerns an incorrigible son.

## 2.1. Offense

The son is portrayed as:

- stubborn/rebellious;
- persistently disobedient after correction;
- gluttonous/drunken;
- not a single-failure case.

Gill reads the text in terms of entrenched disobedience after counsels/admonitions/corrections.

Source:
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/gill/deuteronomy/21.htm

## 2.2. Actor / procedure

Crucially, the parents do **not** execute sentence themselves.

They must:

- agree;
- bring the son to the elders;
- make accusation in the city gate;
- submit the case to public civil authority;
- the men of the city execute the Mosaic civil penalty.

Keil/Delitzsch explicitly notes that the law both **defends and limits parental authority**: the refractory child is taken out of private parental power into public magistrate procedure.

Source:
- https://www.biblehub.com/commentaries/kad/deuteronomy/21.htm

### P0

This text actually cuts against a private-authoritarian use:

> **Parents do not become judge, jury, and executioner because a son rebels.**

The case is transferred to lawful public authority in covenant Israel.

## 2.3. Covenant transfer

Under 1689 LBCF 19.4, Israel's judicial laws as a political body expired with that polity except for their general equity.

Therefore the death penalty is not a Christian parental sanction.

### General equity likely includes

- persistent filial rebellion is morally serious;
- both father and mother matter;
- discipline must not rest on one private angry impulse;
- serious sanctions belong to the proper jurisdiction;
- society/household need not treat entrenched destructive conduct as trivial.

### What it does NOT prove

- every adult rebel must be removed from home;
- parents may use violence;
- age 18 equals the Deuteronomic son category;
- Christian church or family inherits Israel's civil death sanction.

### Additional historical guard

Gill records Jewish interpretation that the law concerned a narrow transitional son category rather than an independently established adult man. This is not itself inspired, but it further warns against casually using the passage as an adult-child eviction proof-text.

Source:
- https://www.christianity.com/bible/commentary/john-gill/deuteronomy/21

# 3. Ezra 9–10 — foreign wives are NOT a modern template for divorcing unbelieving spouses or expelling children

## 3.1. Covenant setting

Ezra addresses post-exilic Israel's breach involving prohibited intermarriage with peoples of the land in a covenant-restoration setting.

The community:

- confesses corporate trespass;
- convenes under rulers/elders;
- investigates over an extended period;
- separates from the unlawful marriages.

This is not a father privately removing a child from his house.

## 3.2. Actor / jurisdiction

- Ezra functions as priest-scribe with imperial/covenantal authority;
- rulers, elders, judges, congregation are involved;
- the issue is Israel's covenant identity and prohibited intermarriage.

Gill's exposition treats the separation as action taken because these marriages were unlawful under Israel's law.

Source:
- https://www.biblehub.com/commentaries/gill/ezra/10.htm

## 3.3. New Testament control is decisive

1 Corinthians 7:12–16 explicitly tells a believer already married to an unbeliever not to divorce merely on account of the spouse's unbelief if the unbelieving spouse consents to live together.

Therefore:

> **Ezra 10 cannot be copied into New Covenant marriage practice.**

Several older dispensational/evangelical commentaries explicitly recognize the contrast between Ezra's covenant-Israel situation and Paul's instructions for an existing mixed marriage.

Source for the contrast:
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/gaebelein/ezra/10.htm

### P0

If Ezra 10 cannot be transferred directly even to the apparently similar subject of mixed marriage, it certainly cannot be turned into:

> `unrepentant adult child → expel from Christian household`.

### Valid moral transfer

- covenant compromise is serious;
- repentance can require costly structural change;
- confession without rectifying what can lawfully be rectified is incomplete;
- large-scale reform requires truthful process, not emotional improvisation.

# 4. Nehemiah 13 — reform, exclusion from office/proximity, and strong covenant governance

Nehemiah 13 contains multiple acts of forceful reform:

- Tobiah's household goods removed from temple chambers;
- Sabbath commerce confronted;
- mixed-marriage/covenant compromise rebuked;
- one priestly descendant allied by marriage to Sanballat is driven away from Nehemiah.

## 4.1. Actor / jurisdiction

Nehemiah is the governor of the restored Jewish community, exercising civil/covenantal authority over public religious order.

The priestly marriage issue also concerns purity/integrity of priestly office and covenant community.

Source:
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/nehemiah/13-28.htm

### Transfer

The text supports:

- leaders must protect entrusted holy/community spaces;
- kinship cannot automatically override qualification/integrity;
- roles/access can be removed when conduct contradicts office/covenant requirements.

This is particularly relevant to the project's **church/youth/choir/role** distinction.

### Not transferable as direct family sanction

Nehemiah's “I chased him from me” is not equivalent to:

> “Every father must physically expel a sexually sinful adult son/daughter from residence.”

Different actor, office, location, offense, and covenant institution.

# 5. Absalom — exile, partial return, unresolved justice, and disastrous fake reconciliation

Absalom is useful not because David provides a simple model, but because the narrative demonstrates how complicated **return without resolved moral reality** can be.

## 5.1. Sequence

After Amnon's murder:

- Absalom flees to Geshur;
- David grieves;
- Joab engineers his return;
- Absalom returns to Jerusalem but is barred from seeing the king's face for two years;
- eventually formal access is restored;
- the narrative then moves into Absalom's political manipulation and rebellion.

Gill notes the awkward intermediate state: Absalom is physically restored to Jerusalem but not fully received into David's presence.

Source:
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/gill/2_samuel/14.htm

### P0

Do not romanticize this as an ideal phased-reconciliation manual.

The larger David/Absalom narrative is full of:

- David's parental weakness;
- kingly justice problems;
- Joab's manipulation;
- unresolved moral disorder;
- later rebellion.

### Strong pastoral observation

Nevertheless, the story graphically illustrates that:

`PHYSICAL RETURN ≠ RECONCILIATION`

and

`ACCESS RESTORED ≠ HEART RESTORED`.

That directly supports the project's category separation:

`RELATION ≠ CO-RESIDENCE ≠ TRUST ≠ FELLOWSHIP ≠ APPROVAL`.

# 6. David's other sons — indulgence is itself a biblical danger

The Davidic household gives repeated examples where paternal failure to confront/discipline contributes to disorder.

Especially relevant texts for separate later use:

- 2 Samuel 13 (Amnon/Absalom);
- 1 Kings 1:5–6 (Adonijah; David had not displeased/rebuked him by asking why he acted thus).

### Transfer

> **Parental tenderness can become sinful passivity when a parent refuses necessary correction because correction is painful.**

This supports the user's concern that “love” cannot mean keeping a destructive child comfortable at any price.

### Guard

The failure of David is not permission for retaliatory harshness. His failures are complex and tied to his own sins and royal household.

# 7. Levitical / purity exclusions — holiness matters, but ritual boundaries are not household eviction rules

The Mosaic law excludes certain persons from camp/sanctuary access for ceremonial impurity or disease.

These texts reveal:

- God's holiness;
- ordered sacred space;
- non-interchangeable levels of access;
- restoration procedures.

But they belong to Israel's ceremonial/sacred-space order.

### P0

They cannot directly prove:

> “An impure/sinful Christian adult child may not live under the same roof as believers.”

The New Covenant church applies holiness through Word, sacraments, discipline, qualification, and moral order—not by recreating Israel's ritual camp boundaries.

### Valid church-series application

They reinforce a canonical pattern already present in NT form:

> **hearing God's Word, ordinary presence, Table, office, platform, and trusted access are not one undifferentiated privilege.**

# 9. A New Covenant bridge: church discipline supplies the closest institutional analogue, not parental exile

When the NT deals with a professing believer who persists in scandalous sin, the clearest institutional response is ecclesial:

- Matthew 18:15–20;
- 1 Corinthians 5;
- 2 Thessalonians 3:14–15;

- church recognition;

They do not authorize parents to execute Mosaic civil sanctions.

### Important family implication

# 10. What actually can justify separate residence today?

A modern Christian case for requiring a competent adult child to live elsewhere should ordinarily rest on a **cumulative present-duty argument**, not one OT expulsion verse.

Possible factors:

1. the person is legally competent/adult;
2. parents have already stated lawful household order clearly;
3. the person refuses that order persistently, not merely imperfectly;
4. the conduct uses the house/resources as infrastructure for serious sin;
5. there is danger/corruption toward minors or vulnerable persons;
6. there is violence, theft, drugs, sexual activity in the home, criminal activity, serious intimidation, or repeated destructive disruption;
7. deception makes ordinary household trust impossible;
8. alternatives/consequences are proportionate rather than retaliatory;
9. civil tenancy/eviction/support law is respected;
10. the purpose is truthful stewardship, not “make him suffer enough to convert.”

### P0

A father may sometimes have strong biblical reasons to say:

> “You cannot continue living here under these terms.”

But the argument should be:

`HOUSEHOLD STEWARDSHIP + PROTECTION + NON-ENABLING + ADULT STATUS + LAWFUL PROCESS`

not:

`EZRA/ISHMAEL/REBELLIOUS SON → therefore eviction`.

---

# 11. What can justify continued co-residence despite grave sin?

The opposite must remain possible.

Continued residence may be prudent when:

- there is genuine openness to correction;
- the person accepts meaningful household conditions;
- younger/vulnerable people can be protected;
- deception is decreasing;
- work/education/treatment/counseling plan is real;
- immediate removal would create disproportionate danger;
- the sin occurs but is being fought rather than institutionally defended;
- parents can maintain truth without the entire household collapsing into surveillance warfare.

### P0

> **Severe sin alone does not generate an automatic biblical eviction command.**

The question is also what the parents are **authorizing, enabling, protecting, and able lawfully to govern**.

# 12. “Drive him out so he repents” is the wrong telos

No OT case gives parents a guaranteed conversion technology.

- Hagar/Ishmael serves covenant promise/typology.
- Deut. 21 is civil judgment.
- Ezra is covenant reform.
- Nehemiah is gubernatorial/covenantal reform.
- exile is divine-national judgment.

Therefore:

> **Separate residence can be an act of stewardship. It must not be sold as a sacrament of regeneration.**

God may use it.

God may not.

The parent must obey in his actual jurisdiction and leave the heart to God.

---

# 13. “Keep him home so he hears truth” is also not an absolute telos

The other slogan is equally unsafe:

> “As long as he lives here, at least he hears Scripture, so any level of household corruption is worth tolerating.”

That ignores:

- responsibility toward younger children;
- responsibility for household order;
- possible normalization of grave sin;
- the adult's own responsibility;
- church/public Word access that may remain even if residence changes.

### Strong synthesis

> **Physical proximity is one good among several goods. It is not the supreme good that cancels holiness, protection, truth, and lawful household government.**

# 15. Product-safe conclusions

1. Scripture knows decisive separation; Christian love is not synonymous with permanent co-residence.
2. The OT repeatedly locates severe sanctions in specific God-given jurisdictions rather than private parental impulse.
3. Deut. 21 especially demonstrates both the seriousness of rebellion and a limitation on private parental power.
4. Hagar/Ishmael is governed by unique Abrahamic covenant revelation and NT typology.
5. Ezra 10 is controlled by Israel's covenant setting and cannot override 1 Cor. 7 for existing mixed marriages.
6. Nehemiah supports serious protection of entrusted sacred/community order but is not a household eviction statute.
7. Absalom warns that physical return and relational restoration are not identical.
8. Separate residence today must be justified by actual present stewardship, safety, law, and non-enabling—not imported Mosaic sanctions.
9. Continued co-residence can also be lawful where truthful order and protection remain possible.
10. Neither eviction nor continued residence regenerates.

# 17. Final synthesis

> **The Old Testament makes holiness and rebellion much more serious than modern therapeutic individualism does. It also makes jurisdiction much more serious than private authoritarianism does. The same Bible that records severe removal repeatedly specifies who may act, under what covenant, with what public authority, and for what offense. Christian parents should learn the seriousness without stealing sanctions that God gave to another office or covenant order.**

For the modern household question:

> **Do not ask first, “Which Bible character threw someone out?” Ask: “What has God actually entrusted to us in this house now? What are we knowingly enabling? Who must be protected? What authority still belongs to us over this competent adult? What does civil law require? Can truthful co-residence continue without making the home infrastructure for defended grave sin?”**

That is a stronger and more biblical argument than proof-text eviction.
