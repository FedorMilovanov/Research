# Parental Culpability vs Adult-Child Responsibility — Eli, Samuel, Ezekiel 18, Cain, Isaiah 1

**Status:** SCRIPTURE-FIRST EXEGESIS / PASTORAL GUARDS / RESEARCH ONLY  
**Date:** 2026-09-07  

---

## 1. The pastoral problem

Parents of a seriously wayward adult child are often driven toward one of two false conclusions.

### Fatalistic excuse

> “He is an adult. His sin is entirely his problem. Nothing parents did or failed to do matters anymore.”

### Crushing omnipotent guilt

> “If my son became sexually immoral, deceptive, profane, or apostate, that proves I must have caused it. If I had parented correctly, this could not have happened.”

Scripture allows neither simplification.

A parent can be genuinely guilty before God for **his own acts and omissions**:

- negligent instruction;
- hypocrisy;
- provocation;
- unjust discipline;
- indulgence;
- knowingly financing evil;
- maintaining a corrupt child in a position the parent has real authority to remove him from;
- failing to protect other children.

But an adult child remains personally responsible for **his own willing sin**.

The parent does not become the moral subject who committed the son’s adultery, lies, theft, pornography, blasphemy, or unbelief.

## 2. Eli — Scripture really does blame a parent for culpable toleration

Key texts:

- 1 Sam. 2:12–36
- 1 Sam. 3:11–14

Commentary controls:

- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/1_samuel/2-29.htm
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/1_samuel/3-13.htm
- Gill: https://biblehub.com/commentaries/gill/1_samuel/2.htm

### What Hophni and Phinehas did

They were not merely private young men making choices outside Eli’s reach.

They were priests who:

- abused sacrificial worship;
- took what did not belong to them;
- used force;
- sexually sinned with women at the tabernacle;
- made the LORD’s offering despised.

### What God says to Eli

1 Sam. 2:29 charges him with **honoring his sons above God**.

1 Sam. 3:13 says:

- he knew their iniquity;
- yet failed to restrain/deal adequately with them.

### Gill’s important historical reading

Gill emphasizes that Eli possessed overlapping forms of authority:

- father;
- high priest;
- judge of Israel.

He should not merely have offered a mild verbal protest; given their corruption in priestly office, he had authority to censure/punish/remove them from office.

### Project conclusion

Eli is a devastating text against the excuse:

> “They are adults; therefore there is nothing I can do,”

**when the parent or church actually retains a specific legitimate jurisdiction over what is happening.**

Examples of analogous modern jurisdictions:

- whose house it is;
- whose money is being supplied;
- who controls a ministry role;
- who is responsible for younger children;
- who can remove someone from choir/youth leadership/trusted access;
- who employs or supervises the person.

### Guard

Eli does **not** prove parents retain unlimited childhood authority over every adult child everywhere.

His culpability is tied to:

- knowledge;
- connivance;
- actual retained authority;
- sacred office;
- failure to act where action was available and required.

## 3. Eli directly strengthens the project’s children/youth/ministry lane

There is a very strong analogical principle here.

Eli verbally objected to his sons’ conduct, yet kept them functioning in a privileged sacred position.

That means **verbal disapproval can coexist with practical endorsement**.

Modern application:

A church may say:

> “We have spoken to him about his sexual immorality and profanity.”

But if it knowingly continues to give him:

- choir/platform representation;
- youth leadership;
- trusted private access to minors;
- visible spiritual status;

then the institution must ask whether its **practice contradicts its rebuke**.

Likewise a parent may say:

> “I told my adult son his lifestyle is wrong,”

while continuing to finance or host the exact arrangement that makes it easy.

### Strong line

> **A rebuke is not necessarily restraint. Eli spoke; God still charged him with culpable toleration.**

Guard: the exact sanctions in Eli’s theocratic/priestly office are not directly transferable to modern families/churches.

## 4. Samuel’s sons — similar adult wickedness without the same explicit parental indictment

Key text:

- 1 Sam. 8:1–5

Sources:

- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/1_samuel/8-3.htm
- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/gill/1_samuel/8.htm

Samuel’s sons:

- did not walk in his ways;
- pursued dishonest gain;
- took bribes;
- perverted justice.

The narrative plainly holds **them** responsible.

Unlike Eli’s case, the text does not contain a divine oracle saying:

> “Samuel honored his sons above Me.”

Some commentators speculate about Samuel’s decision to appoint them or what he knew; the biblical narrative itself does not give the same explicit culpability statement found in 1 Sam. 2–3.

### Project guard

Do not build an argument from silence that Samuel was a perfect parent.

But do observe the canonical difference:

> **Scripture knows how to blame a father for culpable connivance when it intends to do so. It does not simply infer parental guilt from the fact that adult sons became wicked.**

This matters enormously for parents whose grown children abandon the faith or enter gross sin.

## 5. Ezekiel 18 — personal moral responsibility is explicit

Key text:

- Ezek. 18, especially vv. 4, 10–20, 21–32.

Calvin:

- https://ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom23/calcom23.vii.xii.html
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/comment3/comm_vol23/htm/vii.vi.htm

Historical setting:

Israelites in exile use the proverb about fathers eating sour grapes and children’s teeth being set on edge.

Ezekiel refutes their attempt to describe themselves as unfair victims of another generation’s guilt.

The chapter deliberately constructs generational cases:

- righteous father;
- wicked son;
- potentially righteous grandson.

The moral point is personal responsibility before God.

Calvin explicitly notes that the prophet is speaking of persons accountable for their own conduct and says each is judged for his own works.

### Core text

> the soul/person who sins shall die;
> son shall not bear father’s iniquity;
> father shall not bear son’s iniquity.

### Project conclusion

This gives us a firm guard against parental omnipotence:

> **A father is not morally charged with his adult son’s sin merely because he is the father.**

The son’s righteousness cannot be inherited from the father.

The son’s wickedness cannot automatically be imputed back to the father.

---

## 6. Ezekiel 18 does NOT mean parental conduct has no consequences

This is the necessary opposite guard.

Ezek. 18 addresses **personal guilt/judgment**, not a modern debate about whether parenting has formative effects.

Scripture elsewhere plainly teaches:

- parents influence children;
- fathers can provoke (Eph. 6:4; Col. 3:21);
- parents must teach (Deut. 6; Proverbs);
- evil company corrupts;
- examples can be imitated;
- Eli was guilty for his own toleration.

Therefore do not say:

> “Ezekiel 18 proves parents bear no responsibility for how they raise children.”

Correct:

> **The parent bears guilt for the parent’s sin; the son bears guilt for the son’s sin. Their sins can interact causally without becoming the same moral act.**

This distinction is crucial.

## 7. Cain — even perfect divine warning does not mechanically produce obedience

Key text:

- Gen. 4:5–8.

Calvin:

- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom01.x.i.html

Before Cain murders Abel, God directly confronts him.

God:

- questions his anger;
- exposes the moral danger;
- warns regarding sin;
- addresses him before the murder.

Cain nevertheless proceeds into murder.

### What this proves

It does **not** prove human parents should imitate divine speech infallibly.

It does show something theologically decisive:

> **Faithful warning is a real means and real duty, but warning does not possess regenerative power in itself.**

The failure of a hearer to obey does not logically prove the warning was absent or defective.

### Strong pastoral correction

A parent can endlessly torture himself with:

> “If only I had found the perfect sentence, he would have repented.”

Genesis 4 gives no support to such a mechanistic anthropology.

Words matter.

God uses means.

But the sinful heart is not a machine whose output is guaranteed by sufficiently skillful parental input.

## 8. Isaiah 1:2 — fatherly nurture and real rebellion coexist

Key text:

- Isa. 1:2–4.

Commentary hub:

- https://biblehub.com/commentaries/isaiah/1-2.htm

In the prophetic lawsuit God says, in paternal imagery, that He reared/brought up sons and they rebelled.

The immediate referent is covenant Israel, not a manual for nuclear-family parenting.

Therefore strict guard:

- God is not merely one human parent among others;
- Israel’s covenant relation cannot be mapped one-to-one onto a Christian household.

Nevertheless the text devastates a simplistic moral equation:

> “Proper fatherly care always mechanically prevents rebellion.”

The canonical story contains both:

- genuine fatherly care;
- real creaturely/covenantal rebellion.

### Safe inference

> **Rebellion does not by itself prove absence of prior care, warning, privilege, or nurture.**

Do not use this as an excuse to ignore identifiable parenting sin.

## 9. A four-question culpability audit for parents

Instead of asking only:

> “Is my son bad because I failed?”

ask separately:

### Q1. What did I actually do or fail to do?

- Was I hypocritical?
- Absent?
- Provoking?
- Harsh?
- Indulgent?
- Cowardly?
- Did I avoid sexual/lust conversations?
- Did I knowingly ignore evidence?
- Did I make truth relationally impossible to tell?

If yes: **repent of specific parental sin**.

### Q2. What authority/resource do I actually retain now?

- my house?
- my money?
- my car?
- my ministry appointment?
- responsibility for younger siblings?

If retained authority is being used to enable known sin, Eli becomes highly relevant.

### Q3. What is now actually the adult child’s decision before God?

- where he lives;
- what he views;
- whom he sleeps with;
- whether he lies;
- whether he repents;
- whether he believes the gospel.

Parents may warn, refuse participation, and set limits on their own resources.

They cannot make these heart/moral decisions for him.

### Q4. Am I confessing my own real sin—or assuming God’s sovereignty over another person?

The first is repentance.

The second can become false guilt and control.

## 10. Parent repentance after discovery

This project should explicitly allow the possibility that **both generations need to repent**.

Example:

- son: pornography, sexual lies, profanity, theft, deceit;
- father: rage, humiliating speech, neglect, cowardly indulgence, hypocrisy, failure to protect;
- mother: manipulation, lying to cover the son, undermining lawful discipline;
- church: known corruption ignored because the child is useful/popular.

The son’s sin does not excuse the parents.

The parents’ sin does not excuse the son.

### Strong formulation

> **Biblical responsibility is not a zero-sum game. A father can need repentance for how he dealt with a sinful son while the son remains fully guilty for the sin he chose.**

## 11. Application to a secretly departing 18-year-old

Suppose:

- double life is exposed;
- serious conversation occurs;
- adult child secretly leaves;
- parents discover continuing sexual/profane rebellion.

Parents should not instantly conclude either:

### “We caused all of this.”

or

### “He is 18, therefore we have zero responsibility left.”

Instead:

- examine specific parental failures honestly;
- repent/restitute where necessary;
- establish safety/facts;
- stop using resources to facilitate sin where appropriate;
- maintain truthful love and appropriate contact;
- protect younger children;
- recognize adult child’s own moral agency;
- trust God rather than trying to manufacture repentance.

---

## 12. Article-ready core formulations

> **Parents are accountable for their parenting. Adult children are accountable for their sin. Those truths are not enemies.**

> **Eli was condemned not because his sons possessed sinful hearts, but because he knowingly tolerated evil inside a jurisdiction God had actually entrusted to him.**

> **Ezekiel 18 forbids a parent from taking ownership of another adult’s moral guilt; Eli forbids him from using that distinction to excuse his own cowardice.**

> **The question is not, “Can I still control him?” but, “What has God actually entrusted to me now, and am I being faithful with that?”**

> **A parent may need to repent deeply without accepting the fiction that he could have regenerated his child by superior technique.**

---

## 13. Claim guards

Do not write:

- Eli proves all parents must control adult children;
- Samuel’s sons prove Samuel had no parenting failures;
- Ezekiel 18 means upbringing has no influence;
- Isaiah 1 means human parents are equivalent to God;
- Cain proves parental instruction is pointless;
- wayward children prove parental innocence;
- wayward children prove parental guilt.

Do write:

- Scripture distinguishes parental culpability from the child’s personal culpability;
- actual retained jurisdiction matters;
- known enabling can itself become parental/institutional sin;
- faithful means are real but non-regenerative in themselves;
- parents should confess specific sin, not assume omnipotent responsibility for another heart.
