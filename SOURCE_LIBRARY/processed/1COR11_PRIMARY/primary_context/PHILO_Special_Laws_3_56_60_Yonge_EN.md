# Philo of Alexandria, *De Specialibus Legis* (Special Laws) III.54–61 — on the woman accused of adultery (Numbers 5) — excerpts, Yonge translation (public domain)

**Source:** C.D. Yonge, *The Works of Philo Judaeus*, 1854–55, Book 29 ("The Special Laws III"), via Early Christian Writings / Early Jewish Writings. Public domain.
**Date:** Philo fl. early-mid 1st century CE (contemporary with Paul).
**Locator:** Spec. Leg. 3.54–61 (cited in series as 3.56, 3.60).

Greek keywords: ἀκατακάλυπτος τῇ κεφαλῇ (uncovered head), ἡ ἐπὶ τῇ κεφαλῇ κόσμος (head-dress/ornament on the head), σύμβολον αἰδοῦς (symbol of modesty).

---

## (54)

And the woman, having incurred two dangers, one of her life, and the other of her reputation, the loss of which last is more grievous than any kind of death, shall judge the matter with herself; and if she be pure, let her make her defence with confidence; but if she be convicted by her own conscience, let her cover her face, making her modesty the veil for her iniquities, for to persist in her impudence is the very extravagance of wickedness. But if there is a reasonable doubt…

## (56)

And the priest shall take the barley and offer it to the woman, and shall take away from her the head-dress (ἀφελὼν τὴν ἐπὶ τῇ κεφαλῇ κόσμον) on her head, that she may be judged with her head bare (ἀνακεκαλυμμένῃ τῇ κεφαλῇ), and deprived of the symbol of modesty (τῆς αἰδοῦς σύμβολον), which all those women are accustomed to wear who are completely blameless; and there shall not be any oil used, nor any frankincense, as in the case of other sacrifices, because the sacrifice now offered is to be accomplished on no joyful occasion, but on one which is very grievous.

## (57–59) — curse of jealousy (omitted: standard Numbers 5 wording on bitter water)

## (60)

And when all these things are previously prepared, the woman with her head uncovered (ἀκατακαλύπτῳ τῇ κεφαλῇ — *note: same collocation Paul uses in 1 Cor 11:5*), bearing the barley flour in her hand, as has been already specified, shall come forward; and the priest standing opposite to her and holding the earthenware vessel in which are the water and the earth, shall speak thus: (61) "If you have not transgressed the laws of your marriage, and if no other man has been associated with you, so that you have not violated the rights of him who is joined to you by the law, you are blameless and innocent; but if…"

## Notes for the 1 Corinthians 11 series

- Philo uses the very phrase ἀκατακάλυπτος τῇ κεφαλῇ for a married woman whose hair/head-dress is stripped in the judicial ordeal of Num 5:18.
- He calls the woman's head-dress the "symbol of modesty" (τῆς αἰδοῦς σύμβολον), stripped from the accused. This is a near-contemporary Jewish-Hellenistic use of the same Greek vocabulary Paul uses in 1 Cor 11.
- Implication: a 1st-century Diaspora Jew hearing ἀκατακάλυπτος τῇ κεφαλῇ in a ritual/assembly context would likely associate it with the uncovered wife of Num 5 — i.e., a woman stripped of the marital modesty-marker. Strong prima facie background to Paul's language.
- Does NOT prove Paul mandates a fabric veil specifically (hair-up vs. let-down is also a live option, and μίτρα/κόσμος could include styled hair); it DOES prove that "uncovered head" in a ritual setting was culturally loaded with shame/adultery overtones in Paul's Jewish-Hellenistic milieu.
