# Candidate dossier — DUTCH-004 *Den nedrigen christen, Op Psalm 131*

**Dossier ID:** DOSSIER-DUTCH-004
**Status:** research complete for the source question; production package opened as a **draft**
**Activated from:** backlog item `DUTCH-CAND-002`
**Selection note:** see §11 for why this candidate was chosen over the other backlog items.

## Проверяемый вопрос

Is *Den nedrigen christen, Op Psalm 131* (Lodenstein) a sufficiently sourced, sufficiently free, and sufficiently singable text to carry the next production in this project — and if so, exactly what may and may not be claimed about it?

## 1. Original title and spelling

| Witness | Title as printed |
|---|---|
| 1676, p. 74 | *Den Nedrigen Christen, Op Psalm 131.* |
| 1727, p. 74 | *Den Nederigen Christen, Op Psalm 131.* |
| 2005 critical edition, item 32, p. 142 | *Den nedrigen christen, Op Psalm 131.* |

The doubled consonant alternates between the editions (*nedrigen* / *nederigen*); both are the same word. The poem is an expansion of Psalm 131, and the title states that anchor explicitly.

**Incipit:** *Myn Vader die mijn smerte siet* (1676, 1727, and the critical edition agree).
**Opening line in the 2005 register of incipits:** "Myn Vader die mijn smerte siet/" — the register is the reliable way to find the poem inside 350 pages of the collection.

## 2. Author and attribution certainty

**Jodocus van Lodenstein (1620–1677)**, Utrecht pastor 1653–1677, Nadere Reformatie. Attribution is **not in doubt** at any level:

- the poem is printed inside his own collection, prepared in his lifetime (1676, a year before his death);
- the 2005 critical edition prints it as item 32 without attribution caveat;
- the 1971 selection includes it under his name;
- no competing attribution was found.

Attribution: **SUPPORTED, effectively certain** (A1 printed witness in the author's own collection).

## 3. Dates — composition and first publication, kept separate

- **Composition date: UNKNOWN.** Unlike the Rees poems (items 34–37), item 32 carries **no date line** in the 1676 print, no date in the 1727 print, and **no date metadata** on the critical-edition page. Nothing in the acquired sources fixes a year of writing.
- **First publication: 1676**, in *Uyt-spanningen* (Willem Clerck, Utrecht), pp. 74–75.
- The critical-edition introduction places the composition of many of these songs in the practice of writing on Sundays as relaxation after preaching, and identifies the collection as largely written for singing in the Utrecht circle — but that is a general characterisation of the corpus, **not a date for this poem**. Do not turn it into one.

Do not infer a composition date from the publication year, and do not import the 1673–74 dating of the neighbouring Rees group into item 32.

## 4. Source text and exact place of publication

**Primary witness used:** *Uyt-spanningen, behelsende eenige stigtelyke liederen* (Utrecht: Willem Clerck, 1676), **printed pp. 74–75**, via the DBNL diplomatic transcription:
<https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/lode002uyt_01_01/lode002uyt_01_01_0036.php>

The 1676 text as printed (page break marked; the transcription's own abbreviations retained):

> **\[p. 74\]** *Den Nedrigen Christen, Op Psalm 131.*
> *Singt als Prendete la: siet boven pag.29.*
> **I.** _M_ Yn Vader die mijn smerte siet, / Mijn herte siet, / Mijn hert gebiedt, / En al sijn grepen weet: / My dunckt, daar legt het neer, / Daar legt het nedrig neer, / En, dwaal ick, proeft het selve wis, / Of 't nog verheven is.
> **II.** Siet, Vader, of mijn oogen aan / Het hoge slaan, / En hoge staan? / Siet my gebogen gaan. / Mijn hert, mijn cleyn begrip, / Mijn oordeel is een stip, / Ten comt aan 't grondelose niet, / Dat het niet door en siet.
> **III.** Als ick u my genegen sie, / Uw wegen sie, / Verlegen bie / Ick u mijn dienst. En wie / Wie ben ick worm, of maad / (Seg ick) die uwen raad / 't Geheym van uw gena-verbond / Soud peylen tot den grond?
> **IV.** Heb ick my niet in billijckheyd **\[p. 75\]** Gewillicheyd, / En stilligheyd / Ootmoedig neergeleydt! / So nedrig als een kind, / So nedrig als een kind / Dat sig ten vollen vergenoegt / Als 't maar de Moeder mint.
> **V.** Een kindje dat maar suygen kan, / En buygen can, / En tuygen van / Sijn onlust met een traan: / En treckt men 't van de borst, / En treckt men etc. / t' Mag schreyen om des moeders raad / Maar noyt het twisten dorst.
> **VI.** So my Heer uw genade-stroom / Te stade coom; / Of spade coom; / Of sig van my vertreckt: / O! droevigen vertreck! / O! droevigen vertreck! / Nog sal mijn siele swijgen stil / Om dat het Vader wil.
> **VII.** O! Sienders, Priesters, Koningen, / Die woningen, / En kroningen / Hebt van Israels Heer. / Wagt verder op sijn gunst, / Wagt etc. / Want is sijn goedheyd grondeloos, / So is sy eyndeloos.

**Shape:** 7 stanzas. Each stanza is 8 printed lines, of which lines 2–3 are short half-lines that belong together with line 1 as a triple (the Dutch uses *slagrijm*, aaa, in the first three lines — the critical edition's own characterisation). Lines 5–6 are a **deliberate doubling**: either a repeated phrase (*So nedrig als een kind, / So nedrig als een kind*, *O! droevigen vertreck!* twice) or a parallel pair (*Mijn herte siet, / Mijn hert gebiedt*). In two stanzas (V and VII) the print does **not** spell the repetition out — it prints `En treckt men etc.` and `Wagt etc.`, which is a compositor's instruction to repeat the preceding line.

This matters for any lyric reconstruction: the `etc.` is a **repeat sign, not a lacuna**, and not different wording. The 2005 edition resolves it by printing the fuller line twice.

## 6. Biblical allusions and genre

**Genre classification (project vocabulary):** `BIBLICAL_CANTICLE_OR_SCRIPTURE_EXPANSION` — a poetic expansion of Psalm 131 that is nevertheless printed with a **melody direction**, i.e. a text intended to be sung. It is not a metrical psalm in the Datheen/Revius sense: it does not versify the psalm; it expands it into a first-person meditation.

**Anchor:** Psalm 131 (three verses). The correspondences are visible in the text itself:

| Psalm 131 | Poem |
|---|---|
| 131:1a "my heart is not haughty" | I.3 *Mijn hert gebiedt*; the whole opening prayer asks God to test the heart's lowliness |
| 131:1b "nor mine eyes lofty" | II.1–3 *Siet, Vader, of mijn oogen aan / Het hoge slaan, / En hoge staan?* — Lodenstein converts the psalm's **assertion** into a **request for testing** |
| 131:2 "as a weaned child with his mother" | IV.5–8 *So nedrig als een kind … Dat sig ten vollen vergenoegt / Als 't maar de Moeder mint*; V.1–8 the suckling child who may cry but never strive |
| 131:3 "Let Israel hope in the LORD" | VII.5–6 *Wagt verder op sijn gunst* — closing address to Israel's prophets, priests and kings |

The 131:1–2 correspondences are directly demonstrable from the text. The 131:3 mapping of the closing stanza is **my analysis** (the poem turns from first person to a plural address and ends on waiting upon God's goodness); it is consistent with the critical edition's note that the poem is faithful to the psalm's basic thoughts, but that note is B1 and is not the sole support for it.

**Secondary literary context (B1, paraphrased, not quoted):** the 2005 commentary characterises the poem as an expansion of Psalm 131, possibly the precipitate of a sermon, notes that neighbouring psalms (132–135, 139) also supply material, and records that the poem was prized in the 19th century and anthologised widely, with a comparison drawn to Willem Sluiter's versification of the same psalm. This is useful reception history; it is **not** evidence for the text and must be attributed.

**Theological boundary:** the poem's lowliness is *rest*, not self-annihilation — the child is content at the mother's breast, cries but does not strive, and the last stanza grounds stillness in the boundlessness of God's goodness. A modern adaptation must not import the darker "being nothing" register of DUTCH-001/DUTCH-002 and thereby flatten a distinct text.

## 7. Melody — what the source actually says, and what is verified

Printed direction: **`Singt als Prendete la: siet boven pag.29.`**

The chain resolves completely inside the book:

1. Printed **p. 29** = *Simeons Lof-sang* (item 9), whose own heading reads **`Te singen Als: Prendete la.`**
2. So the cross-reference is self-confirming: the tune named *Prendete la* enters this collection at p. 29 and item 32 is sung to it. The same page reference works unchanged in the 1727 edition, which also prints *Simeons Lof-sang* on p. 29 with the same `Als: Prendete la.`
3. The 2005 apparatus states that *Prendete la* is an **unknown** tune designation and that the reference points to the melody of text 9.
3a. **Added 2026-10-25 — why two tune names appear to exist.** The 2005 *Melodie-register* (pp. 663–664) lists texts **9 and 32 twice**: once under `Prendete la` and once under `Gy laat/ dien gy vind rede staan`. The second string is item 9's own **incipit**, not a second tune — the register keys the same melody by its name and by the first line of the poem that introduces it, exactly as it does for the group 26/29/50/65/87/88 under `/Nerea`/`Doet yemant sorg`/`Tien Geboden`/`Reveillez vous`. So «напев `Prendete la` или напев „Gy laat…”» is a **false dichotomy**, the same shape as the old "Bourgeois or psalm 42" error. The tune remains untraced; the confusion is now recorded instead of waiting to be re-derived. See `TUNE_DIRECTIONS_FROM_THE_2005_MELODY_REGISTER.md` § 3.2.

**Independent confirmation: NONE acquired.**
- No notation, no text of a *Prendete la* song, and no identification of its origin is in this project.
- The Dutch Song Database (*Nederlandse Liederenbank*, Meertens/KNAW), the normal authority for Dutch tune names, is **offline as of 2026-10-25**: <http://www.liederenbank.nl/> returns HTTP 403, with a notice that the KNAW security department has taken the site down temporarily. DBNL's per-poem links into it therefore currently dead-end.
- The critical-edition introduction notes Lodenstein drew on popular 16th/17th-century tunes *and* on unidentified contemporary compositions, which is consistent with an untraced tune name but proves nothing about this one.

**Permissible:** "the 1676 print directs the singer to the tune *Prendete la*, i.e. the tune used for *Simeons Lof-sang* on p. 29; the tune's origin is untraced."
**Forbidden:** presenting any modern setting as that tune, or asserting a metre from an unacquired melody.

What the printed cross-reference does give us, legitimately, is **metrical information**: *Simeons Lof-sang* and this poem are singable to one tune, so their stanza shapes are compatible. Collating the two 1676 texts:

- *Simeons Lof-sang* I: `GY laat, dien gy vind rede staan, / Heer, vredig gaan // De vrede-baan, / Na 't woord aan hem gedaan: / Wat heb ick hier voor schat, / Te wagten, boven dat, / Dat heyl, dien Saligmaker, dien / Hier nu mijn oogen sien?`
- The second line is printed with an internal division (`//`), i.e. two half-lines sung within one line.
- This matches item 32's stanzas, where the two short printed lines 2–3 fill exactly that divided line.

So the stanza is **seven sung lines**, the second of which divides into two halves. This is a structural observation from collating two texts under one tune; a full scansion (syllable counts per position, cadence) still requires the notation and remains **OPEN**.

**The rights conclusion for production:** the *historical text* is free to adapt. The *specific modern editions* used to read it are not free to copy. The adaptation must therefore be written from the 1676 wording (public domain), with the critical edition used only as a check on sense — which is exactly what the draft in `SUNO/DUTCH-004_PRODUCTION_PACKAGE.md` does.

## 10. Limits for translation, adaptation and publication

1. Do not call a Russian adaptation a translation. It is an adaptation after a public-domain Dutch text.
2. Do not claim the historical tune, its metre, or period performance practice.
3. Do not reproduce the 2005 text, its notes, or its expanded `etc.` readings; the 1676 `etc.` should be described, not silently resolved in the critical edition's wording.
4. Do not attach a composition date. Say "first published 1676; date of writing unknown".
5. Do not absorb this poem into the "creature = nothing / Jesus = all" theme of DUTCH-001/002; its register is childlike rest.
6. Keep the 1727 punctuation variant of stanza IV visible to whoever drafts the lyric: 1676 exclaims, 1727 questions. A lyric may legitimately follow either, but the choice must be recorded.

## 11. Why this candidate (selection justification)

Checked against the selection gate in `DUTCH_HYMN_BACKLOG.md`, and against the other shortlisted items:

- **Quality of primary witness** — the full text survives in the author's own 1676 collection *and* in an independent 1727 typesetting; the two agree closely, and DBNL lists **no editorial intervention** on these pages. This is the cleanest textual situation in the backlog. By contrast `Niet en al` (no. 54) and `Toe-Sang` (no. 86) are long, and the Rees group carries an unresolved date problem.
- **Historical significance** — a widely anthologised, much-praised lyric (per B1 reception notes), and one of the few texts in the collection that names its biblical anchor in the title.
- **Verifiability of text and melody instruction** — text fully read; tune pointer resolved to a named page and confirmed in two editions. By contrast `De Algenoegsaamheyd`, `Jesus Lijdende Liefde` and `Nieuw-Jaars Gebedt` were only sampled in earlier passes, and `I. Hert-sterckte` still has an untraced tune and an unresolved date dispute in its neighbourhood.
- **Rights** — historical text public domain; nothing in the text depends on a copyrighted modern translation.
- **Musical suitability** — seven compact stanzas, strong rhetorical arc (plea → self-examination → confession of smallness → the child image → submission → doxological close), a built-in doubling device in lines 5–6 that gives a natural hook without importing a foreign refrain, and a register that suits this project's restrained acoustic aesthetic far better than another "nothingness" text.
- **Distinctness from existing releases** — DUTCH-001 is created-things-vs-Christ; DUTCH-002 is self-denial through the Rutgers hymn; DUTCH-003 is a Passion sonnet. Psalm 131's weaned child is a genuinely different subject and a different emotional key.

**Rejected for this pass:** `De Algenoegsaamheyd` (too close to DUTCH-001 thematically — the backlog itself warns against it), `Niet en al` (length and refrain policy undecided), `I. Hert-sterckte` (belongs with the open date investigation in `DOSSIER_HERT_STERCKTE_I_II.md`), `Jesus Lijdende Liefde` (Passion theme would repeat DUTCH-003; text not yet fully read).
