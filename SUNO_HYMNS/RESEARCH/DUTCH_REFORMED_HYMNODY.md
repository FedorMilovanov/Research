# Dutch Reformed hymnody — initial historical map

**Status:** orientation dossier. Use exact primary-source locators before quoting historical wording publicly.

## 1. Public liturgy and devotional song culture

Article 69 of the Church Order associated with the Synod of Dordrecht (1618–1619) restricted the repertoire for public church services. The listed texts included the 150 Psalms of David, Ten Commandments, Lord's Prayer, Apostles' Creed, and biblical canticles of Mary, Zacharias and Simeon. The older song *O God die onze Vader bist* was left to the churches' discretion. Other songs were to be removed from church use or discontinued by suitable means.

This regulates public worship, not all devotional poetry or home singing. The accurate statement is that official liturgical repertoire was deliberately narrow, with specified non-psalm texts allowed and one additional song left to local discretion.

Primary text locator: https://kerkrecht.nl/node/2149/

## 2. Earlier synodical policy

Earlier Dutch Reformed synods had already preferred the metrical Psalter associated with Petrus Datheen. The 1578 Dordrecht church-order material discusses singing Datheen's psalms and leaving aside songs not found in Scripture; 1581 and 1586 synodal history continues the cautious policy. Quote the wording from each year's own source rather than blending decisions into one timeless statement.

Source locator: https://kerkrecht.nl/node/2142/

Datheen's Psalter and free devotional hymns should be catalogued separately. A metrical psalm may be produced as a song, but it is a different textual category from a new devotional hymn or biblical canticle paraphrase.

## 3. Local practice and Utrecht's 1615 hymnbook

A Utrecht tradition produced *Hymni ofte Loff-sangen op de Christelijcke feest-dagen ende andersins* in 1615. Its prefatory matter invokes Ephesians 5:19 — psalms, hymns/praises and spiritual songs — as a biblical rationale for a broader sacred-song collection. This is an important counterpoint to the narrower general synodical rule, but does not prove every congregation adopted that repertoire.

Primary digital text: https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/_hym001hymn02_01/_hym001hymn02_01_0004.php

## 4. Lodenstein's Uyt-spanningen (1676)

The 1676 title describes a collection of edifying songs and other poems. Its parts range across biblical matter and expansions of Scripture/Psalms, spiritual meditations, reflections on events and penitential poems, with an appendix. The title page places the book in Utrecht, printed and sold by Willem Clerck in 1676; its front matter cites Ephesians 5:19.

The volume is not a uniform official church hymnbook. Classify each item individually: a song with a tune direction, psalm expansion, dialogue, spiritual poem, penitential poem or another form. Presence in Uyt-spanningen does not by itself prove permission for or routine performance in official Sunday worship.

First-edition entrypoint: https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/lode002uyt_01_01/  
Critical-edition contents: https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/lode002uyt_02_01/index.php

## 5. Melody and contrafactum

The 1676 text of *Schepselen niet en Jesus al* gives the direction *Singt als: Rosemont waer gy vliedt*. The critical edition associates the melody with a Dutch secular song tradition documented in the *Nieu dubbelt Haerlems lietboeck* (1643). This is an example of a sacred text being assigned a known melody, often called contrafactum.

Documented: the text prints a tune direction, and the critical apparatus identifies an earlier songbook connection. Not thereby documented: that a modern SUNO take reproduces original tempo, instrumentation, arrangement or performance practice.

## 6. Three current projects, three different text categories

- *Schepselen niet en Jesus al*: stanzaic devotional song with a printed melody direction; the critical edition dates the text to November 1661.
- *Hert-sterckte in Jehova* / *Zelfverloochening*: a Lodenstein poem dated 4 January 1674, followed by a distinct seven-stanza Rutgers revision printed as hymn 68 in 1806. The Russian track follows the later hymn tradition, not a verbatim translation of the full 1674 poem.
- *Hy droech onse smerten*: a fourteen-line sonnet by Jacobus Revius, first published in 1630. Its current musical setting is modern unless an original musical source is discovered.

## 7. Limits and next questions

This is an initial map, not a definitive institutional history. Local practice varied; a prescriptive order does not document every congregation's practice. A songbook preface gives its editor's rationale, not universal reception. Future work should compare original synod acts, title pages and tune directions, local consistorial records where available, and scholarship with exact page references.


## 8. Edition and access layers — critical correction

The 1676 first-edition text hosted by DBNL is described as a diplomatic rendering, with omitted parts and an editorial correction log. This makes it useful as a primary-source witness, but not a substitute for checking a page image when the claim depends on exact typography, punctuation or a corrected date.

The later 2005 Ros/Strengholt critical edition has its own numbering and editorial apparatus and is explicitly marked as copyright-protected by DBNL. Some critical-edition item numbers group multiple original texts; some repeated portions or stanza elements are supplied for the melody. Keep the 1676 item, 2005 edited item and any modern adaptation linked but distinct.

The accessible DBNL page for the Utrecht Hymni songbook refers to the 1967 facsimile by Jan van Biezen and Marie Veldhuyzen of a work first printed in 1615. Cite it as the 1967 facsimile when describing the object consulted; do not call it an independent digital scan of the 1615 original.

The date of II. Hert-sterckte in Jehova is specially constrained: the accessed editorial record shows 4 January 1674, but says the original print erroneously gave 1664 and that the modern digital text emends it. Keep this particular claim on EVIDENCE_HOLD until the original page image has been checked.
