# DUTCH-005 — transmission chain and the tune that changed

**Run:** 2026-10-26, autonomous source pass. **Question:** where does *Heerlijckheyds Loff* go after 1676, and what happens to its melody between the print and the 1938 hymnal?

**Result in one line:** the text was reprinted at least four times before the twentieth century, and the tune direction changed from an untraceable `Un jour &c.` in 1676–1681 to **Psalm 42** by 1718 — which is the melody the 1938 hymnal prints, credited there to Louis Bourgeois. The old open question "Bourgeois or psalm 42" is therefore not a contradiction: it is one tune under two names.

| 1806 | *Evangelische Gezangen*, XLIII, p. 82 | *Zielverheffing tot Jezus*; «Hoog, omhoog, het hart naar boven!» | melody named `Psalm 042 Datheen` | DBNL p. 82 read in full |
| 1938 | *Psalmen en Gezangen*, Gezang 75, p. 501, nr. 239 | «'t Oog omhoog, het hart naar boven» | melody credited **Bourgeois, Louis** | database rendering; text also in a public reproduction |

Two things follow, and both are usable in copy:

1. **The Rutgers reworking is not a paraphrase of the title only.** It rewrites the opening address: the soul of 1676 («mijn siel») becomes the eye and the heart («het hart naar boven»), and the stanza is recast into eight lines instead of nine. That is recorded in the 2005 apparatus as "in door A. Rutgers gewijzigde vorm", and the 1806 page shows the result.
2. **The tune moved while the text stayed.** In 1676 and 1681 the direction still points at `Un jour`; in 1718 and 1806 it points at Psalm 42; the 1938 hymnal names Bourgeois. Bourgeois is the composer to whom the Genevan psalm 42 tune is credited, so "psalm 42" and "Bourgeois" describe the same melody, not two rivals.

## 2. The 1806 page, read rather than summarised

DBNL, *Evangelische Gezangen* (1806), [p. 82], hymn XLIII:

> Hoog, omhoog, het hart naar boven! / Hier beneden is het niet: / 't Ware leven, lieven, loven / Is maar, daar men Jezus ziet. / Wat men hoort' of zie op aard', / Is ons kostlijk hart niet waard: / Wil men leven, lieven, loven; / Hoog, omhoog, het hart naar boven!

Read against the 1676 stanza I, this is a compression: the threefold `op aard` of the original is gone, the closing `niet waard` is kept but moved, and the last line turns the poem's opening line into the stanza's refrain. **So the "refrain" that a modern ear associates with this hymn is a Rutgers feature, not Lodenstein's** — which is exactly why the Russian adaptation keeps the original's repeat structure and does not borrow the 1806 shape.

## 3. The 2005 apparatus, in full, on the melody

DBNL, *Uyt-spanningen* (2005), item 56, [p. 263], marginal note to the tune line:

> Melodie: Herkomst onbekend, anders dan *Un jour &c*, van tekst 27.

Translated: the melody's origin is unknown, and it is not the *Un jour &c.* that stands over text 27. The same edition, [p. 267], adds the transmission sentence:

> Deze zeer bekende tekst is in door A. Rutgers gewijzigde vorm op de melodie van psalm 42 opgenomen in verschillende gezangenbundels.

So the editors themselves separate two objects: the **1665 tune**, which they could not trace, and the **later psalm 42 melody**, on which the reworking circulated. Copy must keep them apart in the same sentence, or a reader will hear "psalm 42" as a solution to the 1665 question.

## 6. What this changes in the copy

- The description gains the tune shift (1681 → 1718) as a fact with a named witness family, and drops the implication that psalm 42 has nothing to do with the hymn's history at all.
- The open defect «мелодия 1938: Bourgeois или 42-й псалом» is **closed as a false dichotomy**, not resolved by preference.
- Nothing about the 1665 tune gets stronger: it remains untraced, and the Russian adaptation still uses a modern musical setting, not a historical one.
