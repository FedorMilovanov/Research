## 2. What the poem does

**The shape is the argument.** Almost every stanza runs the same two-part movement: first the earthly cost — loss, labour, insult, estrangement, hunger, mockery — then the turn, in a closing couplet, that all of it is borne for Jesus' sake. The 2005 headnote calls this the poem's structural signature and reads the repetition itself as the theme: the cross-bearing is **voluntary**, and the poem proves it by refusing to complain without also rejoicing. That is this project's own paraphrase of the editorial note, not a quotation of it.

**The opening stanza sets the trade in one image.** The poet says his song rises not from bitterness but from gain: for one pound of cross and grief, a thousand pounds of joy. Stanzas II–XIII then enumerate what the cross actually costs a person in a Dutch town in 1659 — property taken, slander, hard labour, fasting, sleeplessness, friends estranged, a household watching. Stanza XIV makes the hardest turn: even when the *watchman* himself — the image is the Song of Songs, the watchmen who strike the bride and take her veil — mocks his complaint, he will bear it. Stanza XV closes by naming the exchange: he despises wealth, rest and the lusts of the flesh, because Christ is his treasure, rest, desire, hope, comfort and honour.

**The "refrain" is the print's own, and it is not mechanical.** This matters for any future adaptation, because the channel forbids *added* refrains. Nothing would need to be added here — but nothing may be filled in either:

| Stanzas | Closing couplet as handled by the print |
|---|---|
| II–XI | the couplet is given in full in II–V, then **abbreviated to `Dat is om uwent wil, O! etc.`** in VI–XI |
| XII–XIII | the verb changes: `Dat wil ick **dulden**` — "suffer, endure" instead of `lijden` |
| XIV | the frame breaks: `Dat lijd ick al om **u**` — the plural address of the crowd becomes a singular address to Christ |
| XV | the couplet is not repeated at all; its second line returns as the poem's last statement |

So the print itself varies the refrain four ways and then **abbreviates it with `etc.`** for six stanzas. The project's rule that the print's `etc.` is not to be expanded editorially (see `LESSONS_AND_GUARDS.md` and the rights rule on the 2005 edition) applies here before any adaptation is even considered: an adapter who "restores" six full couplets is inventing text the edition declined to print in full.

## 3. Tune

- 1676 and 2005 both print `'t En is niet alle tijd, &c.` with a page reference to **p. 100**, which is item 41, *Jesus lijdende liefde*. The 2005 melody register lists `'t En is niet alle tijd, &c.` under texts **41 and 57** — consistent, and recorded in `TUNE_DIRECTIONS_FROM_THE_2005_MELODY_REGISTER.md` § 2.
- The 2005 headnote adds the identification that matters: that incipit is the opening line of **Camphuysen's *Christelijcke Gelatenheydt***, and the note reads Lodenstein as working in its wake. The headnote also lists the poem's literary dossier (Van Es; Buijnsters; Trimp; Zwaan; De Vrijer, who prints four stanzas).
- This is a **named song**, not a notation. Nothing in this project holds Camphuysen's melody, and the Song Database that would index it is offline. What the direction gives, legitimately, is: this text and item 41 are singable to one tune, so their stanza shapes are compatible — the same structural inference DUTCH-004's dossier draws from its cross-reference.

**Permissible in copy, if this ever goes to production:** "the 1676 print sends the singer to the tune of Camphuysen's *Christelijcke Gelatenheydt*, the same tune as *Jesus lijdende liefde* on p. 100; the melody itself has not been collated."
**Forbidden:** calling the tune "found", printing a modern setting as if it were the 1659 melody, or deriving a metre from a melody nobody here has seen.

## 4. Textual notes — 1676 against the 2005 edition

Read in full on both sides on 2026-10-25; differences are listed because a future adaptation must follow the print, not the modern text:

| Place | 1676 | 2005 | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| I.4 | `wel duysend ponde n vreugd` | `wel duysend ponden vreugd!` | transcription artefact: the italic `n` of *ponden* is detached in the DBNL text layer. Print reads *ponden* |
| V.4 | `als 't u **blieft**` | `als 't u belieft` | 1676 has the shorter form here and the longer everywhere else — either a compositor's slip or genuine variation. **Unresolved**, and it does not affect sense |
| XI.3 | `verschop, bespott, veragtt` | `verschopt, bespott, veragt` | spelling/past-participle ending only |
| XIV.1 | `**Ia** dat de wagter self` | `Ja dat de wagter self` | `Ia` is the print's long-I spelling of *Ja* |
| XV.4 | `Mijn schat, mijn rust, mijn lust, mijn hoop, mijn troost,` | `… mijn troost/ mijn Eer.` | **the 1676 transcription stops short**: *mijn eer* is missing from the text layer, and the closing couplet's rhyme is incomplete without the following line. `EVIDENCE_HOLD` — needs the page image of printed p. 160 |

The last row is the reason this dossier is not marked "text verified in full". A transcription that loses the rhyme word of the final line cannot be used as the base for an adaptation, and no page image of pp. 158–160 has been inspected (same access limit as DUTCH-005; see `COLLATION_DUTCH-005_THREE_WAY.md` § 5).

## 5. Relation to what the channel has already released

- **Against DUTCH-003 (*Hy droech onse smerten*, Revius):** no collision. Revius's sonnet is about **Christ's** suffering; this poem is about the **disciple's** cross-bearing, and it quotes Christ's own call to take up the cross rather than describing the Passion. The two would read as a pair, not a repeat.
- **Against DUTCH-005:** no overlap in argument. DUTCH-005 turns the soul upward toward the heavenly life; this poem stays on earth and works out what following Christ costs here.
- **Against item 41, *Jesus lijdende liefde*:** the same tune and an adjacent theme — which is precisely why the version of this text printed in an earlier session's notes as "the cross hymn" must not be confused with item 41. They are two poems, two numbers, one tune.
