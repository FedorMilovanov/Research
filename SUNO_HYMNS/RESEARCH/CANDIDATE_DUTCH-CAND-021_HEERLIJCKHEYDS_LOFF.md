# Identification — *Heerlijckheyds Loff* ("Hoog, om hoog…")

**Question:** is the Lodenstein text worked on in earlier sessions as `Heerlijckheyds Loff / Hoog, omhoog…` a separate work, a variant of an already catalogued text, or part of another composition?

**Answer: a separate work, now identified.** Its bibliographic identity is resolved; it is **not** a variant of any existing catalogued text, and it is **not** part of another composition. It is registered as backlog candidate `DUTCH-CAND-021` — a *candidate*, not a production track.

**Dossier opened:** 2026-10-25 (fourth pass). No track ID, no lyric, no prompt and no asset has been created for it.

## Проверяемый вопрос, answered

The earlier uncertainty was legitimate: the work was known only from Russian stanzas VII–X and a style brief, with no title page and no locator. The text is in the 1676 collection at a fixed locator, and every identifying feature in the working notes matches it.

**It is not in the catalogue.** `DUTCH-001`, `DUTCH-002` and `DUTCH-003` are *Schepselen Niet en Iesus Al*, *II. Hert-sterckte → Zelfverloochening*, and *Hy droech onse smerten*. *Heerlijckheyds Loff* shares stanzas and vocabulary with DUTCH-001's poem — both organise themselves around `leven / lieven / loven` and the worthlessness of earthly things — but they are **different poems with different titles, dates, tune lines and stanza counts**. The resemblance is Lodenstein's idiolect, not identity.

## Why the earlier identification failed, and what settled it

The working notes recorded only Russian stanzas **VII–X**, described as addressing Beauty and the soul's ascent. That description matches the actual stanzas VIII–IX of this poem almost exactly:

- **VIII**: *Alderminnelijckste Schoonheyd! / Oorsaack van wat hier schoon schijnt* — the address to Beauty, "cause of whatever seems beautiful here".
- **IX**: *Allerley Bevallijckheden / Diemen hier bedencken can … Sijnder maar een schaduw van* — all loveliness is only a shadow of it.
- **X**: the doxological close — *Ah! dat aller menschen tongen, / Aller Engelen wackerheyd / t'Samen-spanden, t'samen songen / Iesus lof en Heerlijckheyd!*

So the Russian stanzas came from the poem's closing third. That correspondence is why the identification can be stated positively rather than as a guess.

**The Russian stanzas themselves are not in the repository.** Their wording is therefore unverified, and this dossier makes no claim about their accuracy.

## Structure — a note for any adaptation

The stanza is **not** a simple strophic unit. Each has a long opening, then an abbreviated half-line that the print marks with `etc.` (a repeat instruction, not a lacuna), then a **tripled word** that changes across the poem:

| Stanzas | Tripled figure |
|---|---|
| III | *dit heerlijck leven, leven, leven* |
| IV | *Sonder sterven leven, leven, leven* |
| V | *Sonder sonden leven, leven, leven* |
| VI | *Vangt der deugden leven, leven, leven* |
| VII | *Daar ick Iesus lieven, lieven, lieven* |
| VIII | *Wil ick oyt yets lieven, lieven, lieven* |
| IX | *Lust my yet te lieven, lieven, lieven* |
| X | *Iesus is te loven, loven, loven* |

This progression — **leven → lieven → loven** — is the poem's architecture, and it is the one thing an adaptation must either reproduce or explicitly discard. It is an *internal* device; it is not a chorus to be detached, and it is not a licence to invent a refrain. A setting that replaces it with an added chorus has replaced the poem's spine.

## Melody

Printed direction: *Voyse: Un jour &c.*

The 2005 apparatus states (B1, paraphrased): the melody's **origin is unknown**, and it is **different from** the *Un jour &c.* tune used at text 27 of the same collection. So the print's tune name is a designation that the modern editors could not resolve to the same tune, and the melody is untraced. **No notation, no identification and no recording is held.** As with the rest of this corpus, the Dutch Song Database is the normal authority and was offline on 2026-10-25.

## Transmission — the poem became a well-known hymn in a reworked form

This is the most consequential finding for the project, because it creates a **rights and attribution trap**:

| Witness | Form |
|---|---|
| 1676, pp. 155–158 | *Heerlijckheyds Loff*, 10 stanzas |
| Stapel, *Lusthof* (1681), p. 495 | reprinted as *Heemels heerlijkheids lof* |
| *Bundeltje* (1718), p. 230 | reprinted as *HEERLYKHEITS LOF*, with a recorded stanza scheme |
| *Hervormde Bundel* (1938) | **a different poem in modernised form**: *'t Oog omhoog, het hart naar boven!*, with a melody attributed to **Loys Bourgeois** |

The 1938 item is a **reworking by a twentieth-century hymnal editor**, with its own melody attribution — not the 1676 text and not the 1665 tune. It is the form most likely to be familiar to a modern listener, and therefore the one most likely to be mistaken for the historical poem. Any future work on this text must state which form it follows. The 1938 wording and melody carry their own rights questions.

## A related 1938 attribution, and what it can and cannot prove

In the fifth pass the printed 1938 Hervormde Bundel was collated for Gezang 205 (see `COLLATION_GEZANG205_THREE_WAY.md`). That page establishes the bundel's **layout convention**: the composer's name is printed above the item and the text author's below it — e.g. Gezang 204 carries `Louis Bourgeois († 1510—1572)` above and `Johannes Helder (1834—1933)` below; Gezang 202 carries `William Croft` above and `Johann Andreas Böhringer` below.

That convention is the mechanism by which a 16th-century tune name becomes attached to a 20th-century hymnal item, and it is consistent with the earlier report that *'t Oog omhoog, het hart naar boven!* appears in the 1938 bundel with Bourgeois as its melody.

**But it does not verify that item.** `'t Oog omhoog` was **not located** in the printed bundel in this pass. The claim stays at `SUPPORTED_BY_SECONDARY_SOURCE_NEEDS_COLLATION`, resting on the Kerkliedwiki listing and the bundel's demonstrated layout. Do not cite the Bourgeois attribution as print-verified until the page is read.

## Music brief recorded in earlier sessions

Male monastic ensemble and epic organ. **Status: `DISCUSSED_NOT_APPROVED`, and a duplication risk.** An essentially identical brief (male monastic polyphony, powerful organ, epic character) also appears in the notes for **DUTCH-002**. Since no verbatim prompt for either work is in this repository, it cannot now be determined whether one prompt was reused for two different poems. When prompts are recovered, they must be matched to the poem they were written for — a prompt is not transferable evidence that the right text was sung.
