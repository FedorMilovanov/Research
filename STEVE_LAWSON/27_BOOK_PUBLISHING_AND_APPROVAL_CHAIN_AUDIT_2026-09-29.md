## 1. Bottom line

`Mercy in the Wilderness: How I Fell & What I Found` is strongly established as a Lawson-authorized publication. The remaining uncertainty is not whether the book exists or whether Lawson stands behind it; it is **how it was produced, who exercised editorial/pastoral oversight, and whether the family/church/counselor approval standard described in early 2025 was applied to the book**.

The public publishing metadata strongly suggests a **private, author-controlled, or self-publishing-like imprint**, but the corpus does not yet establish the legal owner of `But God Press`.

Canonical classification:

> **BOOK AUTHORSHIP/AUTHORIZATION: VERY STRONG.**

## 4. Metadata anomaly strengthens, but does not prove, self-publishing inference

Most retail feeds identify `But God Press` as publisher.

However, AbeBooks currently carries at least one edition/listing whose metadata says:

- publisher: `Steven Lawson`

while another listing for the same ISBN names `But God Press`.

Source:

- https://www.abebooks.it/products/isbn/9798996167302

Possible explanations include:

1. author-owned imprint / self-publishing metadata;
2. reseller metadata normalization/error;
3. a distributor record in which author and imprint fields were conflated;
4. different upstream feeds for the same POD ISBN.

Therefore the supported wording is:

> **A self-publishing or author-controlled imprint is strongly suggested by the POD model, one-title footprint, and author/publisher metadata overlap, but legal ownership of But God Press remains unverified.**

Do not state “Lawson is But God Press” as fact without an ISBN-agency/business/contract record.

Evidence supporting Lawson authorization includes:

- multiple primary/near-primary contacts cited by journalists/reviewers;
- Adam Markley's claim that, after he contacted Stephens Valley Church, he received a direct response signed by Steven J. Lawson confirming he wrote the book;
- reviewers with primary contacts independently confirming authenticity;

Again, because the email itself is not shown, use only with attribution:

If authentic, this is conceptually important: it means the book is not merely an incidental devotional title but functions as Lawson's chosen extended public account.

## 9. Monetary-motive guardrail

The book is commercial and POD/self-publishing-like, and a self-controlled publishing arrangement can yield different economics than a traditional contract.

But the public corpus does not establish:

- actual unit sales;
- print cost;
- royalty per copy;
- who receives proceeds;
- whether proceeds are donated, used for family support, or retained;
- whether financial motive drove publication.

Therefore:

- `money grab` = opinion, not fact;
- “he is profiting from his sin” should not be asserted without actual proceeds/economic evidence;
- prudence of monetization may be criticized separately from motive.

## 10. Why the private-imprint structure matters even without accusing motive

The question is editorial accountability.

With a traditional Christian publisher, one can often identify:

- publisher/editor;
- theological review;
- marketing framing;
- contractual process;
- institutional responsibility for publication.

For `Mercy in the Wilderness`, the current public record does not reveal comparable editorial governance.

That is especially relevant because the book is presented as a spiritually instructive confession after a major public moral collapse.

A private imprint is not inherently improper. But it makes the question “who challenged, edited, fact-checked, and pastorally reviewed this narrative?” more important, not less.

## 11. Current article-ready formulation

> Lawson's book does not appear to have come through the ordinary publishing network that handled his earlier ministry books. Retail data identifies `But God Press`, a public imprint footprint that currently appears to consist essentially of this single POD title; one AbeBooks metadata feed even lists `Steven Lawson` as publisher for the same ISBN. That is not enough to prove Lawson legally owns the imprint, but it strongly suggests an author-controlled or self-publishing-style project. The more consequential question is pastoral rather than commercial: in early 2025 Lawson's allies said even a short public statement was being held until family, counselors, church elders, and OnePassion leaders judged it appropriate. No public source yet shows whether those same people reviewed or approved the 2026 book. If the book is now Lawson's chosen extended public statement, knowing who exercised meaningful editorial and pastoral oversight is part of evaluating the testimony itself.
