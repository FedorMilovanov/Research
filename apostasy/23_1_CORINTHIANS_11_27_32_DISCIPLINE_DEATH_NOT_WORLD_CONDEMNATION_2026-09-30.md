В корпусе уже встречаются страшные земные суды:

> **смертельный Божий суд = доказанное вечное осуждение.**

Павел сам запрещает такое автоматическое равенство.

---

## 2. Контекст: злоупотребление трапезой Господней — не мелкая литургическая ошибка

1 Кор. 11:17–34 описывает собрание, которое фактически унижает церковь:

- divisions;
- богатые/имеющие едят раньше;
- один голоден, другой пьян;
- презрение к church of God;
- shame toward those who have nothing;
- unworthy participation in Lord’s supper.

Следовательно, “unworthy manner” — прежде всего **способ участия**, выражающий презрение к Христову телу/общине, а не требование достигнуть внутренней безгрешной “достойности” прежде Причастия.

---

## 3. `ἔνοχος` body/blood + `κρίμα` — суд реален

v.27:

> unworthy eater/drinker becomes accountable/guilty concerning body and blood of Lord.

v.29:

> eats/drinks `κρίμα` to himself, failing to discern body.

Это серьёзный judicial vocabulary. Павел не говорит, что Евхаристия/Вечеря безопасна при сознательном презрении к церкви и Христу.

Но `κρίμα` ещё не должно автоматически переводиться как **final condemnation** — v.32 сам отличает present Lord’s judgment/discipline от `κατακρίνω` with world.

---

## 4. v.30 — Павел прямо связывает грех с физической слабостью, болезнью и смертью

`διὰ τοῦτο ἐν ὑμῖν πολλοὶ ἀσθενεῖς καὶ ἄρρωστοι καὶ κοιμῶνται ἱκανοί.`

> Поэтому многие среди вас слабы и больны, и немало **спят**.

Greek context:
- https://biblehub.com/bgb/1_corinthians/11.htm

Modern scholarship notes Pauline `κοιμάομαι` is used metaphorically for **death**, not ordinary sleep, in his letters.

A useful NTS article surveys this and the physical-illness/death setting:
- https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/new-testament-studies/article/abs/physical-weakness-illness-and-death-in-1-corinthians-1130-deprivation-and-overconsumption-in-pauline-and-early-christianity/956A2001833AB92DE591CC9629E6AD7F

### High-confidence result

Some Corinthians have actually **died** in connection with divine judgment/discipline surrounding the Supper.

That makes v.32 especially important.

---

## 5. v.31 — self-judgment as means of avoiding judgment

`εἰ δὲ ἑαυτοὺς διεκρίνομεν, οὐκ ἂν ἐκρινόμεθα.`

If we judged/discerned ourselves, we would not be judged.

The logic is not fatalistic:

> “If God preserves, self-examination is irrelevant.”

Rather, Paul commands discernment/self-judgment as a **means** of avoiding disciplinary judgment.

This belongs alongside:

- 1 Cor 10:12–14 watch/flee;
- Heb 3 mutual exhortation;
- Jude keep/rescue;
- James 5 turn back wanderer.

---

## 6. v.32 — the decisive distinction

Greek:

`κρινόμενοι δὲ ὑπὸ τοῦ κυρίου παιδευόμεθα, ἵνα μὴ σὺν τῷ κόσμῳ κατακριθῶμεν.`

> Being judged by the Lord, **we are disciplined**, so that **we may not be condemned with the world**.

Greek witness:
- https://biblehub.com/texts/1_corinthians/11-32.htm
- https://biblehub.com/text/1_corinthians/11-32.htm

### Lexical contrast

- `κρίνω` — judge;
- `παιδεύω` — discipline/train/chasten;
- `κατακρίνω` — condemn.

The purpose/result clause `ἵνα μὴ... κατακριθῶμεν` establishes a theological distinction **inside the verse**:

> Lord’s present judgment can function as discipline precisely **so that His people are not condemned with the world**.

This is one of the clearest texts against treating every severe judgment, even death, as evidence of final damnation.

---

## 7. Does Paul include even those who “sleep” under the protective purpose?

The natural flow vv.30–32 strongly includes the whole disciplinary phenomenon:

1. because of sin, weakness/sickness/death;
2. self-discernment could avoid judgment;
3. when judged by Lord, **we** are disciplined so as not to be condemned with world.

Thus it is reasonable to see even physical death as potentially falling within divine corrective/protective judgment rather than eternal condemnation.

### Guardrail

The text does not authorize us to identify **which modern illness/death** is divine discipline. Paul has revelatory apostolic diagnosis of Corinthian situation; we do not.

Do not weaponize this passage against sick or bereaved people.

---

## 8. Calvin — paternal judgment vs destructive condemnation

Calvin explicitly emphasizes v.32 as consolation:

- God can be angry with believers without forgetting mercy;
- chastisements serve their welfare;
- punishment is paternal rather than destructive;
- purpose: they not be condemned with world.

Primary source:
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom39.xviii.iv.html
- alternate edition: https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/comment3/comm_vol39/htm/xviii.iv.htm

This is historically important because it shows classic Reformed perseverance **does not deny frightening temporal judgments**; it classifies them under fatherly discipline/preservation.

## 9. Relation to wilderness deaths in 1 Cor 10

Only one chapter earlier many Israelites are physically destroyed in wilderness.

1 Cor 11 now gives a control:

> physical destruction/death can be a severe covenantal judgment whose eternal significance cannot simply be inferred from death event alone.

Therefore when interpreting 1 Cor 10:

- real destruction remains warning;
- but not every dead Israelite’s final eternal destiny can be read directly off bodily death.

This strengthens the earlier dossier’s distinction between **historical judgment** and **final damnation**.

---

## 10. Relation to Ananias and Sapphira

Acts 5 gives sudden deaths inside church setting but does not state final eternal destiny.

1 Cor 11 supplies a Pauline category showing why restraint is warranted:

> God may impose death within covenant/church life as disciplinary judgment without that act being identical to condemnation with world.

This does **not prove** Ananias/Sapphira were regenerate and saved; it proves that **death itself is insufficient evidence for the opposite conclusion**.

---

## 11. Relation to Hebrews 12 discipline

Heb 12:5–11 develops fatherly discipline of sons:

- whom Lord loves He disciplines;
- discipline marks sons;
- painful now, fruit of righteousness later.

1 Cor 11:32 gives an even sharper eschatological purpose statement:

> discipline functions so as not to be condemned with world.

These should be cross-linked in flagship’s section on **discipline vs apostasy**.

---

## 12. Relation to “sin unto death” in 1 John 5:16

There is an obvious canonical analogy: sin can be associated with death within Christian-community discourse.

But 1 John 5:16 is itself difficult:

- physical vs eternal death;
- regenerate vs false professor;
- intercessory limits.

Do not solve one hard text by assuming the disputed solution of another.

Use 1 Cor 11 as the **clearer control**: here physical death and non-condemnation with world are directly adjacent.

## 14. What text clearly proves

High confidence:

1. Christian assembly can incur real divine judgment.
2. Such judgment can include weakness, sickness and death.
3. Paul calls Lord’s judging action `παιδεία`/discipline.
4. He explicitly distinguishes this from being condemned with world.
5. Self-examination/discernment is a means for avoiding judgment.
6. Therefore severe temporal judgment is not a reliable shortcut to final-soteriology verdict.

---

## 15. What text does NOT prove

It does not prove:

- every church member who dies under discipline was regenerate;
- no Christian sin has any eschatological seriousness;
- church warnings are only about physical health;
- every illness is chastisement for a specific sin;
- ministers today can diagnose God’s hidden reason for death;
- final perseverance makes discipline unnecessary.

---

## 16. Current synthesis for flagship

This text should become the **primary control** whenever article discusses scary temporal judgments:

> Scripture itself distinguishes a Lord’s judgment that can reach physical death from the world’s final condemnation. God may judge His people severely **in order not to condemn them with the world**.

That allows the article to preserve both truths:

- “God’s judgments inside church are frighteningly real”;
- “we must not pronounce eternal damnation merely from temporal catastrophe.”
