## 1. Главный вопрос

1 Ин. 5:16–17 различает:

- `ἁμαρτίαν μὴ πρὸς θάνατον` — грех не к смерти;
- `ἁμαρτία πρὸς θάνατον` — грех к смерти.

Иоанн связывает первый случай с молитвой и дарованием `ζωή`, но о втором говорит:

> `οὐ περὶ ἐκείνης λέγω ἵνα ἐρωτήσῃ`

«не о том говорю, чтобы он просил».

Основные модели:

1. физическая смерть верующего как крайняя Божья дисциплина;
2. окончательная духовная/вечная смерть, связанная с отступничеством и антихристологическим отрицанием;
3. особая категория закоренелого/хульного отвержения Бога, близкая по характеру к хуле на Духа;
4. комбинированная модель, в которой physical judgment может быть частью более широкой death-category.

Цель — определить не просто «какая модель удобнее для perseverance», а какая лучше согласуется с **самим словарём 1 Иоанна**.

# 2. DIRECT EXEGESIS — греческий текст

1 Ин. 5:16:

`Ἐάν τις ἴδῃ τὸν ἀδελφὸν αὐτοῦ ἁμαρτάνοντα ἁμαρτίαν μὴ πρὸς θάνατον, αἰτήσει, καὶ δώσει αὐτῷ ζωήν, τοῖς ἁμαρτάνουσιν μὴ πρὸς θάνατον. ἔστιν ἁμαρτία πρὸς θάνατον· οὐ περὶ ἐκείνης λέγω ἵνα ἐρωτήσῃ.`

1 Ин. 5:17:

`πᾶσα ἀδικία ἁμαρτία ἐστίν, καὶ ἔστιν ἁμαρτία οὐ πρὸς θάνατον.`

Direct Greek:
- https://www.greekbible.com/1-john/5

### Observations

1. `ἁμαρτία πρὸς θάνατον` is **anarthrous** — John does not write “the specific sin” with an article.
2. `πρός + accusative` expresses direction/result: sin **toward / resulting in death**.
3. The contrast is not “small sin vs large sin,” because v.17 immediately insists:
   > all unrighteousness is sin.
4. The distinction concerns **destination/outcome**, not a Roman-style taxonomy of venial versus mortal sins by intrinsic size.

### DIRECT EXEGESIS conclusion

The phrase most naturally denotes a **category/state/trajectory of sin whose terminus is death**, not necessarily one uniquely named act such as murder, adultery or suicide.

---

# 3. “Brother” does not automatically identify the mortal-sin subject as regenerate

The first clause says:

> if anyone sees **his brother** sinning a sin not leading to death…

Then John shifts:

> there is sin leading to death…

He does **not** explicitly say:

> “if anyone sees his brother committing the sin unto death.”

### Why this matters

A physical-death-of-believer reading often argues:

> “John says brother; therefore both categories concern regenerate believers.”

But grammatically `brother` directly identifies only the first case — the one **not** leading to death.

The mortal category is introduced more generally.

### DIRECT EXEGESIS conclusion

The verse does not prove that the person committing sin unto death is a regenerate brother.

Nor does it prove the opposite merely by the grammatical shift.

This question must be decided by broader Johannine context.

---

# 4. The immediate context strongly defines `ζωή` as eternal life

The strongest internal control lies immediately before v.16.

## 5:11

God has given us `ζωὴν αἰώνιον` — eternal life — and this life is in His Son.

## 5:12

- whoever has the Son has **the life**;
- whoever does not have the Son does not have **the life**.

## 5:13

John writes so believers may know that they have **eternal life**.

Then vv.14–15 discuss confident prayer.

Then v.16 says prayer results in God giving the sinner `ζωήν`.

### Contextual pressure

It is therefore difficult to assume without argument that `ζωή` in v.16 abruptly means only:

> continued biological existence.

### Johannine-wide controls

1 John 3:14:

> we have passed from **death to life** because we love the brothers.

1 John 5:12:

> having the Son = having life.

1 John 5:20:

> Christ is the true God and eternal life.

### Project weighting

`ζωή / θάνατος` in this immediate discourse is primarily **soteriological/eschatological**.

Physical-life reading remains possible but carries the burden of showing an intentional semantic switch.

# 5. The physical-death discipline model

## John MacArthur

MacArthur consistently reads “sin unto death” as physical death of a true believer under extreme divine discipline.

He appeals to:

- Acts 5 — Ananias and Sapphira;
- 1 Cor. 11:30–32 — weak, sick and some “sleep” because of abuse of the Lord’s Table;
- church-purity judgments;
- the possibility that God removes a persistently sinning believer from earthly life without loss of salvation.

Direct GTY:
- https://www.gty.org/sermons/1301-G/bible-questions-and-answers-part-9
- https://www.gty.org/sermons/80-142/purging-sin-from-the-church
- https://www.gty.org/sermons/48-30/understanding-christian-freedom
- https://www.gty.org/sermons/44-20/when-sin-entered-the-church-part-2

### Strengths

1. Scripture undeniably contains physical-death discipline of covenant/church members.
2. 1 Cor. 11:30–32 is particularly strong because Paul explicitly says such judgment prevents condemnation with the world.
3. It explains why prayer cannot remove a decreed temporal judgment.
4. It takes church-purity discipline seriously.

### Major pressure points

1. Immediate 1 John context defines life as eternal life.
2. “brother” directly modifies only the non-death case.
3. John’s major polemic concerns antichristological secession, not primarily physical discipline of faithful believers.
4. The letter repeatedly contrasts life/death as spiritual realms.

### Weight

`SERIOUS CONSERVATIVE CANONICAL OPTION / MEDIUM LOCAL-CONTEXT WEIGHT`.

It must not be presented as obvious from the Greek.

# 6. The secessionist / final-apostasy reading

This reading interprets the death-category within the main crisis of 1 John.

## 2:19

The secessionists:

- went out from the apostolic community;
- manifested that they were not truly “of us.”

## 2:22–23

The liar/antichrist denies:

- Jesus is the Christ;
- the Father and the Son.

Whoever denies the Son does not have the Father.

## 4:1–3

Spirits/teachers are tested by confession of Jesus Christ come in flesh.

## 5:10

One who does not believe God’s testimony about His Son makes God a liar.

## 5:11–12

Having/not having the Son defines possession/non-possession of life.

### CANONICAL/BOOK-LEVEL INFERENCE

“Sin unto death” fits naturally as **Christ-rejecting, persevering unbelief/apostasy that places a person in the realm of death because he does not have the Son**.

This is much more Johannine than searching for an unrelated secret list of deadly acts.

# 9. Calvin — apostasy, not a single ordinary fall

Calvin’s exposition is unusually close to the current book-context reading.

Primary:
- https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom45.v.vi.v.html?scrBook=1John&scrCh=5&scrV=16
- https://biblehub.com/library/calvin/commentaries_on_the_catholic_epistles/1_john_5_16-18.htm

Calvin argues:

- “sin unto death” is not a partial fall or breach of one commandment;
- it is apostasy/defection that wholly alienates from God;
- children of God do sin, but do not wholly abandon God and surrender finally to Satan;
- fatal defection manifests reprobate abandonment;
- blasphemy against Spirit belongs to the same kind of hardened defection.

### Pastoral guardrail from Calvin

He explicitly warns against rashly declaring people hopeless.

Because mortal apostasy is difficult to know and God’s mercy is abundant, love should incline us to hope rather than prematurely pronounce eternal death.

This is highly valuable for final pastoral application.

# 10. Is this identical to blasphemy against the Holy Spirit?

Not necessarily.

### Similarities

Both categories concern:

- hardened rejection after light;
- a condition associated with no forgiveness/restoration;
- deep repudiation of divine testimony.

### Differences

The Synoptic blasphemy texts arise from a very specific historical accusation:

> Jesus’ Spirit-wrought works are attributed to Satan.

1 John 5 does not narrate that accusation.

### Better formulation

> The two may belong to the same **terminal hardening family**, but 1 John 5 should not be reduced to a direct synonym unless stronger evidence is supplied.

Yarbrough’s “umbrella rubric” approach is therefore preferable to exact identity.

---

# 11. Why John says “I do not say that he should pray”

John’s wording is carefully restrained.

He does **not** use the strongest explicit prohibition formula:

> “I forbid prayer.”

He says:

> concerning that, I am not saying that one should ask.

### Possible significance

This may reflect:

- recognition that certain apostasy cases lie outside the normal promise of restorative intercession;
- pastoral restraint about cases where final hardening has become manifest;
- refusal to extend vv.14–15’s prayer-confidence mechanically to rejection of the Son Himself.

### OT canonical background

Jeremiah is at times told not to pray for a people whose rebellion has reached announced judgment:

- Jer. 7:16;
- 11:14;
- 14:11.

This supports the category of **judicially ripened rebellion**, though John does not explicitly quote Jeremiah.

### Guardrail

Because believers today lack prophetic omniscience, the church must be extremely cautious in deciding that a living sinner is beyond prayer.

---

# 12. 5:18 immediately follows and strongly matters

1 John 5:18:

> everyone born of God does not continue in sin; the one born of God is kept, and the evil one does not touch him.

The precise aspect/meaning of `οὐχ ἁμαρτάνει` must be read with the whole letter; John has already said believers do sin (1:8–2:2).

### Rhetorical effect after vv.16–17

John distinguishes:

- sin occurring within the life of the regenerate, for which intercession/restoration is appropriate;
- a deathward kind of sin;
- the new-birth reality in which the evil one does not finally possess the one born of God.

### CANONICAL/SYSTEMATIC pressure

This sequence strongly supports the Reformed reading that **final deathward apostasy manifests non-regeneration rather than loss of regeneration**.

Calvin explicitly reads the sequence this way.

### Restriction

This remains an inference from discourse and theology, not the lexical meaning of `πρὸς θάνατον` by itself.

# 14. Why the text does not teach Roman Catholic mortal/venial taxonomy

John says:

> `πᾶσα ἀδικία ἁμαρτία ἐστίν` — all unrighteousness is sin.

His distinction is not:

- this list of inherently light sins;
- that list of inherently mortal sins;
- mortal sin loses justification by a quantified category.

Rather, the epistle contrasts:

- sin within the sphere of life/confession/restoration;
- sin whose direction/outcome is death because it belongs to settled rejection of God’s Son/light/truth.

Therefore any later systematic taxonomy must not be read backward into John’s phrasing.

# 15. Strong conditional-security reading

A conditional-security interpreter may argue:

1. John says “brother”;
2. a brother can commit sin unto death;
3. God gives life in response to prayer for lesser sins;
4. therefore a regenerate believer can sin his way into spiritual death.

### Main weakness

The grammatical structure does not explicitly call the mortal-sin person “brother.”

More importantly, 1 John 2:19 and 5:18 provide strong retrospective/preservation controls:

- final secession manifests non-belonging;
- the one born of God is kept.

### Verdict

The conditional-security reading is possible only by importing an identification the sentence itself does not state and by explaining why 2:19 / 5:18 do not function as preservation controls.

`COMPETING VIEW — WEAKER THAN IN HEB 6 / HEB 10 / 2 PET 2`.

# 17. Preferred project synthesis

> **First John 5:16–17 does not identify one secret act that automatically damns a Christian. The Greek speaks more generally of “sin toward death,” and the immediate context overwhelmingly uses life and death as Johannine soteriological categories: God has given eternal life in His Son; whoever has the Son has life; whoever does not have the Son does not have life. The letter’s central crisis is likewise the secession of antichristological teachers who deny the Son and thereby show that they were not truly “of us.” For that reason the reading represented by Calvin, Yarbrough, Kruse and Jobes—that sin unto death is settled, unregenerate, Christ-rejecting apostasy or conduct belonging to that realm—best fits the letter as a whole. MacArthur’s physical-death interpretation remains a serious conservative alternative because Scripture does contain lethal discipline of believers in Acts 5 and 1 Corinthians 11, but it requires a less natural shift from the immediate eternal-life vocabulary. The mortal sin should not be equated mechanically with one occurrence of the Synoptic blasphemy against the Spirit, though both belong to the wider biblical category of hardened rejection after great light.**

# 18. Pastoral theology

## 18.1. Most sinning believers are not to be treated as terminal apostates

John’s first command is intercession.

If a brother sins not unto death:

> pray.

This aligns with:

- Gal. 6:1;
- James 5:19–20;
- Jude 22–23.

The church’s reflex should be rescue, not pronouncement of reprobation.

## 18.2. Tender conscience is evidence against settled deathward apostasy

A person who:

- confesses sin;
- seeks Christ;
- fears separation from Him;
- wants forgiveness;
- submits to apostolic truth

does not resemble the secessionist/antichristological pattern that dominates 1 John.

## 18.3. Do not pronounce “sin unto death” casually

Calvin’s warning is excellent:

> because God’s grace is rich and final hardening is difficult for us to know, love should incline us to hope rather than rashly conclude eternal death.

## 18.4. Yet do not trivialize apostasy

John does recognize a category in which sin is directed toward death and normal intercessory assurance is no longer promised.

The church must therefore resist both:

- despair over ordinary/grievous but repentable sin;
- false assurance to hardened Christ-denial.
