# 2. Exact Greek control

Luke 8:13:

`οἱ δὲ ἐπὶ τῆς πέτρας οἳ ὅταν ἀκούσωσιν μετὰ χαρᾶς δέχονται τὸν λόγον, καὶ οὗτοι ῥίζαν οὐκ ἔχουσιν, οἳ πρὸς καιρὸν πιστεύουσιν καὶ ἐν καιρῷ πειρασμοῦ ἀφίστανται.`

Core clauses:

- `μετὰ χαρᾶς δέχονται τὸν λόγον` — receive the word with joy;
- `ῥίζαν οὐκ ἔχουσιν` — they have no root;
- `πρὸς καιρὸν πιστεύουσιν` — they believe for a time;
- `ἐν καιρῷ πειρασμοῦ ἀφίστανται` — in a time of testing they depart/fall away.

NET correctly notes that `πειρασμός` here is better heard as **testing/trial** than reduced to inner temptation-to-sin, and explicitly calls the faith temporary.

Public technical witness:
https://classic.net.bible.org/verse.php?book=luk&chapter=8&verse=13

### Methodological rule

No single lexical item decides the whole soteriology:

- `πιστεύω` must not be weakened into “did not believe at all”;
- `ἀφίστημι` must not be inflated into the technical proposition “lost regeneration/justification”;
- `ῥίζα` is an image, not a lexical synonym for regeneration;
- the theological judgment must come from the **whole contrast** among vv.12–15.

# 3. Luke’s own internal contrast remains decisive

## 3.1. First soil — v.12

`ἵνα μὴ πιστεύσαντες σωθῶσιν`

Luke directly joins believing and being saved.

Therefore the project must never pretend that `πιστεύω` in v.13 is semantically empty.

## 3.2. Rocky soil — v.13

The same narrative now qualifies belief by four features:

1. joy;
2. no root;
3. temporary duration;
4. falling away under testing.

## 3.3. Good soil — v.15

The good soil:

- hears;
- holds fast (`κατέχουσιν`);
- bears fruit;
- does so `ἐν ὑπομονῇ` — with endurance/perseverance.

NET renders the contrast explicitly as “cling to it” and “bear fruit with steadfast endurance.”

Source:
https://classic.net.bible.org/passage.php?passage=Luk+8%3A13-15

### Exegetical center

The question is therefore not:

> “Was the verb believe used?”

but:

> **What kind of believing response does Luke distinguish from the enduring, fruitful response that reaches the parable’s positive goal?**

# 4. Robert H. Stein — conservative Baptist exegetical control

Robert H. Stein, *Luke*, New American Commentary 24, p. 245, is one of the strongest modern conservative controls because he directly addresses the salvation question rather than merely describing agriculture.

His argument, preserved with an exact electronic/print locator in multiple citation trails, is:

- the first soil is plainly unsaved because v.12 says the word is removed so that they do not believe and be saved;
- the final/good soil represents the positive saving response;
- despite “receive the word with joy” and “believe,” the second and third soils belong with the failed soils;
- for Luke, faith/hearing has saving significance only as it **endures and produces fruit**;
- the emphasis is therefore not on a one-time profession but on perseverance.

Locator witness:
https://puritanboard.com/threads/parable-of-the-sower-denies-perseverance-of-the-saints.41022/

Citation preserved there:
Robert H. Stein, *Luke*, NAC 24 (Nashville: Broadman & Holman, 1992; electronic 2001), p. 245.

# 5. Darrell Bock — lexical/contextual technical control

Darrell L. Bock, *Luke 1:1–9:50*, BECNT, discusses the unit around pp.735–736.

A Duke dissertation that interacts directly with Bock identifies those pages and notes that Bock understands Luke’s `πειρασμός` in the line of the Synoptic persecution/testing context.

Locator witness:
https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/80ec5dd8-d4a6-426d-91be-c67b3b9e590e/content

The same secondary discussion importantly challenges Bock at one point: Luke’s vocabulary may broaden testing beyond Matthew/Mark’s persecution language because `πειρασμός` and `ἀφίστημι` form literary links to Luke 4:13 and Satanic conflict.

### Why this matters

We should **not** reduce `πειρασμός` to only one external form.

The safest synthesis is:

> Matthew/Mark explicitly emphasize tribulation/persecution because of the word; Luke’s `πειρασμός` is broader and can encompass the decisive testing in which allegiance to the Word is exposed.

## 6.2. NIGTC locator

> “therefore regeneration/justification was certainly possessed and then lost”

still requires a theological step beyond the lexical data.

### Source-policy judgment

Marshall is valuable as an academically serious Greek-aware interlocutor even where his perseverance conclusions differ from the site’s confessional stance.

# 7. John Calvin — primary Reformed passage commentary

Calvin’s Synoptic commentary is unusually useful because he refuses both simplifications.

Primary accessible text:
https://biblehub.com/commentaries/calvin/luke/8.htm

Calvin says, in substance:

- temporary faith is a real “vegetation” of the seed;
- such people can eagerly receive the Gospel and appear promising;
- Luke calls it belief because their response **resembles faith** and they in some manner believe;
- nevertheless they are not truly regenerated by the incorruptible seed that abides;
- true faith has a different permanence because of the Spirit of adoption.

### Critical distinction

Calvin does **not** solve the text by saying:

> “Jesus says believe, but actually they do not believe at all.”

His category is more precise:

> **temporary faith is faith in a real/general sense, but not saving/regenerating faith in the specific sense.**

This is directly compatible with the project’s anti-gloss rule.

### Product custody

The online Calvin text is usable for research and short attribution, but exact edition/volume/page should still be normalized before publication-quality quotation.

# 8. John Owen — temporary faith “true in its kind”

Owen gives an even more exact systematic category in *The Doctrine of Justification by Faith*.

Accessible primary text:
https://www.godrules.net/library/owen/120owen_a2.htm

Owen distinguishes degrees/kinds of assent and says temporary faith can:

- operate strongly on the affections;
- produce real changes in life;
- resemble saving faith to a considerable degree;
- yet fail under opposition and never bring a person to eternal rest.

His crucial category is that temporary faith is:

> **true in its kind**, not merely falsely named faith,

while **not of the same special nature as justifying faith**.

An accessible PDF edition of Owen’s *Justification by Faith* likewise contains the temporary-faith discussion:
https://www.reformedontheweb.com/owen/justification-by-faith.pdf

### Why Owen is stronger than the slogan “false faith”

“False faith” can mislead readers into imagining conscious hypocrisy or zero real response.

Owen permits a more text-sensitive formulation:

> real illumination/assent/affection/change can occur without the specific Spirit-wrought faith that unites to Christ savingly and perseveres.

### Relation to Luke 8

Owen’s formulation is a theological synthesis, not a lexical definition of `πιστεύω`.

Use it **after** Luke’s own qualifiers.

# 9. MacArthur / GTY — trials expose rather than destroy saving faith

Primary GTY source directly on Luke 8:13:

John MacArthur, “Receptivity to the Gospel, Part 2,” sermon 42-104, Luke 8:13, May 5, 2002:
https://www.gty.org/sermons/42-104/receptivity-to-the-gospel-part-2

Related trial/perseverance exposition:
https://www.gty.org/sermons/59-6/from-trouble-to-triumph-part-3

MacArthur’s main argument:

- the response can look strongly positive: hearing, receiving, joy, belief;
- the crucial diagnosis is `no root`;
- trial does not destroy genuine saving faith but exposes the kind of response already present;
- true faith is shown by endurance; temporary response collapses under cost/testing.

He repeatedly uses Luke 8:13 elsewhere in GTY as a paradigm of temporary/professing faith rather than regenerated faith that later loses justification.

Additional GTY controls:
https://www.gty.org/sermons/81-143/christian-deconstruction-part-1
https://www.gty.org/sermons/43-46/the-freedom-of-true-discipleship

### Guardrail

MacArthur sometimes speaks homiletically of “false faith” or “half-converted” people.

For the research corpus, Owen/Calvin’s more exact language is preferable:

> **real temporary faith/response in its kind, but not saving faith of the rooted/persevering kind.**

This preserves MacArthur’s soteriological conclusion without flattening Luke’s verb.

# 10. Confessional Lutheran reading — primary evidence, not caricature

The *Formula of Concord*, Solid Declaration XI.42, explicitly cites Luke 8:13.

Primary text:
https://bookofconcord.org/solid-declaration/election/

It says that many:

- receive the Word with joy;
- afterward fall away again;
- do not fall because God is unwilling to give persevering grace;
- but because they willfully turn away, grieve/resist the Holy Spirit and return to the world/sin.

It also connects this with:

- Phil. 1:6;
- 2 Pet. 2;
- Eph. 4:30;
- Heb. 10:26;
- Luke 11:25.

### What must be represented fairly

The Lutheran reading is **not**:

> “every serious sin instantly erases salvation.”

Its confessional logic is closer to:

> genuine faith and Spirit-work may be resisted/abandoned through willful return to unbelief/sin; God is not deficient in grace, but apostasy is genuinely possible.

### Pressure on Reformed reading

This is a serious competing model because it does not deny:

- divine initiative;
- Spirit’s work;
- need for perseverance;
- Phil. 1:6.

Its dispute with Reformed perseverance concerns whether one in whom saving work genuinely began can finally self-abandon that faith.

### Pressure back on Lutheran reading

Luke 8:13 itself still does not explicitly say:

- justified;
- regenerated;
- Spirit-indwelt;
- adopted.

Those identifications are supplied from the Lutheran doctrinal synthesis, not from the word `πιστεύω` alone.

# 11. Wesleyan / Arminian layer — distinguish sources carefully

The existing primary dossier rightly separates Arminius from later Wesleyan dogmatics.

## 11.1. John Wesley’s note is not enough by itself

Wesley’s short note on Luke 8 adds little detailed exegesis to v.13, although on v.15 he explicitly highlights **perseverance** as the contrast with the stony soil.

Accessible text:
https://biblehub.com/commentaries/wes/luke/8.htm

Therefore Wesley should not be inflated into a technical Greek authority here.

## 11.2. Marshall is the stronger technical interlocutor

Marshall’s dedicated Tyndale Bulletin study and NIGTC discussion supply the serious language/context pressure for a conditional-perseverance reading.

## 11.3. Arminian inference must still be named as inference

A strong Arminian/Wesleyan argument can be stated:

1. v.12 joins belief with salvation;
2. v.13 explicitly says the second group believes;
3. it receives the Word positively;
4. `ἀφίστανται` describes departure under testing;
5. therefore the most straightforward reading may be that genuine faith has been abandoned.

The key disputed step is #5’s equation:

`genuine faith` = `regeneration + justification already possessed`.

The Reformed response need not deny 1–4. It denies that 1–4 lexically require that equation, especially against:

- `no root`;
- temporariness;
- fruitlessness;
- the positive enduring contrast of v.15.

# 12. Free Grace pressure remains distinct from Arminian pressure

Do not merge Free Grace and conditional-security readings.

Free Grace typically reasons:

- v.13 belief is saving belief;
- eternal life was therefore acquired irrevocably;
- later failure is loss of discipleship/fellowship/reward, not eternal life.

This shares with Arminian readings the premise that the rocky-soil faith is saving in origin, but reaches the opposite conclusion about final security.

### Diagnostic value

Because two opposed systems can both begin:

> “`πιστεύουσιν` must equal saving faith,”

and then diverge sharply, the project should resist making the whole debate turn on the bare verb.

The qualifiers are exegetically indispensable.

# 13. Lexical hardening — `ἀφίστανται`

`ἀφίστημι` can mean:

- depart;
- withdraw;
- desert;
- fall away;
- in religious contexts, apostatize.

Luke 8:13 unquestionably describes a **real departure** from the prior believing response.

But lexical meaning alone does not say:

> “a once-regenerate person has become unregenerate.”

The verb describes movement away; the soteriological status of the one moving must be established from context/theology.

### Lucan literary observation

Luke also uses the same verb family for departure in non-soteriological movement, so a doctrine cannot be extracted from morphology alone.

A useful literary observation from current scholarship is that Luke 8:13’s `πειρασμός` / `ἀφίστημι` can echo Luke 4:13’s temptation conflict and Satan’s departure, broadening “testing” beyond only persecution.

Technical discussion with Bock locator:
https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/80ec5dd8-d4a6-426d-91be-c67b3b9e590e/content

# 14. “No root” is more determinative than emotional intensity

The rocky soil:

- hears;
- receives;
- receives with joy;
- believes.

If conversion were diagnosed from **initial intensity**, this would look excellent.

Jesus instead points beneath the visible reaction:

> `ῥίζαν οὐκ ἔχουσιν`.

### Pastoral implication

Evangelistic assurance should not be manufactured from:

- a raised hand;
- a prayer formula;
- strong tears;
- immediate joy;
- baptism alone;
- a short period of zealous activity.

Nor should the church cynically despise such responses.

The biblical task is:

- nurture the Word;
- teach the whole counsel of God;
- prepare converts for cost/testing;
- look for continuing faith and fruit;
- restore those who stumble rather than instantly classify them as terminal apostates.

---

# 15. Peter remains the crucial anti-abuse control

Luke 22:31–32 prevents a cruel misuse of Luke 8:13.

Peter:

- enters severe testing;
- denies Christ three times;
- fails publicly and shamefully.

Yet Jesus says He prayed:

> that Peter’s faith may not fail,

and anticipates:

> “when you have turned again, strengthen your brothers.”

### Therefore

The difference between:

- temporary faith ending in final unbelief;
- true faith suffering grievous collapse and restoration

is **not** simply the severity of one visible episode.

The diagnostic horizon includes:

- Christ’s preservation;
- return/repentance;
- continuing allegiance;
- eventual fruit.

This belongs with James 5:19–20, Gal 6:1 and Jude 22–23 in the restoration matrix.

---

# 16. Calvin and Owen vs crude “fake faith” language

For the final article, avoid the phrase:

> “they never really believed anything.”

It conflicts with Luke’s wording and is less precise than the best Reformed tradition.

Preferred formulation:

> **They truly believe in a temporary/general religious sense: the Word is really received, joy is real, assent is real, and observable change may be real. But the response lacks the root, endurance and fruit that characterize saving faith. In Calvin/Owen’s categories, temporary faith is real in its kind without being the specific justifying faith wrought and preserved in the elect.**

This formulation:

- honors `πιστεύουσιν`;
- honors `no root`;
- avoids psychological accusation of conscious hypocrisy;
- preserves classical Reformed perseverance.

# 20. P1-3 closure verdict

**P1-3 Luke 8:13 is architecturally CLOSED.**

Final research synthesis:

> **Luke refuses two shortcuts. He does not let us say the rocky soil “never believed in any sense,” because he explicitly says they believe for a time. Nor does he let us equate the bare verb `πιστεύω` with proven regeneration/justification, because Jesus immediately qualifies the response: no root, temporary duration, falling away under testing, and no persevering fruit. Classical Reformed theology is strongest here when it follows Calvin and Owen: temporary faith can be real in its kind without being the specific justifying faith that God roots and preserves. Lutheran and Arminian readings rightly force the Reformed interpreter to take the positive language at full strength, but the additional conclusion that justification/regeneration was possessed and then lost is not supplied by the verb alone. The good soil remains Luke’s own positive control: it holds the Word and bears fruit with endurance.**
