# Иакова 5:19–20 — заблуждение, возвращение, спасение от смерти и покрытие множества грехов

## 1. Главный вывод

Иак. 5:19–20 не позволяет церкви относиться к заблудшему человеку как к уже окончательно потерянному:

> `Ἀδελφοί μου, ἐάν τις ἐν ὑμῖν πλανηθῇ ἀπὸ τῆς ἀληθείας καὶ ἐπιστρέψῃ τις αὐτόν...`

Иаков предполагает реальную возможность, что **кто-то среди адресуемой общины** уйдёт от истины, и реальную обязанность другого человека **вернуть его**.

Результат возвращения описан предельно серьёзно:

> `σώσει ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ ἐκ θανάτου καὶ καλύψει πλῆθος ἁμαρτιῶν`
>
> «спасёт его душу/жизнь от смерти и покроет множество грехов».

Рабочая экзегетическая оценка после source-hardening:

1. наиболее естественный referent `αὐτοῦ` — **заблудший/грешник**, которого возвращают;
2. `ψυχή` сама по себе не означает технически «бессмертную нематериальную душу» — может означать человека/жизнь;
3. `θάνατος` в 5:20 лучше всего читается на фоне Иак. 1:15, 1:21 и 4:12 как **окончательная/эсхатологическая смерть**, хотя физическая смерть остаётся серьёзной competing view и может входить в более широкий death-complex;
4. `καλύψει πλῆθος ἁμαρτιῶν` наиболее естественно связано с **прощением/не-вменением грехов возвращённого**, а не с человеческой заслугой спасателя;
5. обращение `ἀδελφοί μου` и фраза `ἐν ὑμῖν` устанавливают ecclesial proximity, но **не доказывают автоматически**, что каждый возможный wanderer ранее был возрождён/оправдан;
6. столь же ошибочно заранее объявлять такого человека terminal apostate: сам Иаков предписывает **искать возвращения**;
7. doctrine of perseverance не отменяет restoration ministry: предупреждение, увещание и возвращение являются средствами, через которые Бог сохраняет и восстанавливает Своих и через которые ложное исповедание может быть приведено к действительной вере.

# 2. Exact Greek — 5:19

Greek:

`Ἀδελφοί μου, ἐάν τις ἐν ὑμῖν πλανηθῇ ἀπὸ τῆς ἀληθείας καὶ ἐπιστρέψῃ τις αὐτόν`

Key forms:

- `Ἀδελφοί μου` — «братья мои»;
- `ἐάν τις ἐν ὑμῖν` — «если кто-нибудь среди вас»;
- `πλανηθῇ` — aorist subjunctive passive от `πλανάω`, быть уведённым/заблудиться;
- `ἀπὸ τῆς ἀληθείας` — прочь/в сторону от истины;
- `ἐπιστρέψῃ` — aorist subjunctive active от `ἐπιστρέφω`, повернуть/вернуть;
- `τις αὐτόν` — кто-нибудь [вернёт] его.

Greek witnesses:
- https://biblehub.com/text/james/5-19.htm
- https://greekbible.com/james/5/19

### Lexical guardrail

`πλανάω` + `ἐπιστρέφω` описывают **directional movement** относительно истины.

Нельзя сделать из самих глаголов полный ordo salutis:

> «раз человек “вернулся”, значит он непременно был regenerate, потерял regeneration и получил её снова».

Но нельзя и ослаблять текст:

> «это всего лишь небольшое интеллектуальное недоразумение».

Ст. 20 называет путь `πλάνη ὁδοῦ` и связывает его со смертью и множеством грехов.

# 3. “Among you” — ecclesial proximity, not automatic regeneration proof

Иаков говорит не о далёком язычнике, о котором община ничего не знает:

> `ἐν ὑμῖν` — среди вас.

Это создаёт сильную ecclesial presumption:

- человек находится внутри поля христианской общины;
- знает/исповедует истину достаточно, чтобы от неё уклониться;
- другие христиане несут ответственность за его возвращение.

Но New Testament repeatedly distinguishes:

- visible association;
- profession;
- saving reality.

И сам Иаков в 2:14–26 различает professed faith и faith, действительно проявляющуюся как живая.

Kevin McFadden (Detroit Baptist Theological Seminary) подчёркивает, что Иаков различает professed/spurious faith и genuinely saving faith; dead faith не является второй полноценной saving-faith category.

Source:
https://dbts.edu/2023/09/09/the-nature-of-saving-faith-in-james/

### Поэтому

`among you` ≠ `proven regenerate individual`.

Но `among you` также запрещает холодную пастырскую реакцию:

> «раз ушёл — значит никогда не наш, не нужно искать».

Иаков говорит прямо противоположное: **верните его**.

# 4. Exact Greek — 5:20

Critical-text form:

`γινωσκέτω ὅτι ὁ ἐπιστρέψας ἁμαρτωλὸν ἐκ πλάνης ὁδοῦ αὐτοῦ σώσει ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ ἐκ θανάτου καὶ καλύψει πλῆθος ἁμαρτιῶν.`

Key forms:

- `γινωσκέτω` — пусть знает;
- `ὁ ἐπιστρέψας` — тот, кто возвратил;
- `ἁμαρτωλόν` — грешника;
- `ἐκ πλάνης ὁδοῦ αὐτοῦ` — из заблуждения его пути;
- `σώσει` — спасёт, future active;
- `ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ` — его душу/жизнь/person;
- `ἐκ θανάτου` — от смерти;
- `καλύψει` — покроет;
- `πλῆθος ἁμαρτιῶν` — множество грехов.

Greek witnesses:
- https://biblehub.com/text/james/5-20.htm
- https://www.biblegateway.com/verse/grc/James%205%3A20

NET makes the referent explicit in translation: the Greek is “his soul,” with “his” taken as the sinner just mentioned.

Source:
https://classic.net.bible.org/verse.php?book=Jam&chapter=5&tab=commentaries&verse=20

# 5. Textual variants — important but not decisive for the death question

Two visible variation points:

## 5.1. `γινωσκέτω` vs `γινώσκετε`

- singular imperative: «пусть он знает»;
- plural form: «знайте» / «вы знаете» depending punctuation/function.

Modern critical traditions substantially support `γινωσκέτω`.

This changes the address of the exhortation slightly, but **not the nature of death**.

## 5.2. `ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ` vs `ψυχήν`

Some textual traditions omit the explicit `αὐτοῦ` after `ψυχή`.

Comparison:
https://biblehub.com/texts/james/5-20.htm

### Effect

The variant matters for referent discussion, but even where `αὐτοῦ` is absent the sentence naturally continues to concern the sinner just returned.

No major variant changes `ἐκ θανάτου` into a different object.

### Publication status

- basic variant awareness: `GREEN`;
- full NA28/ECM manuscript weighting: `AMBER` unless needed for quotation apparatus.

# 6. Whose soul/life is saved?

Historically several combinations have been proposed:

1. rescuer saves **his own** soul and covers his own sins;
2. rescuer saves his own soul and covers wanderer’s sins;
3. rescuer saves wanderer’s soul but covers his own sins;
4. rescuer instrumentally saves wanderer’s soul and the wanderer’s sins are covered.

The fourth is currently the strongest reading.

Reasons:

- immediate object is `ἁμαρτωλόν`;
- `ὁδοῦ αὐτοῦ` already refers naturally to that sinner;
- where explicit `ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ` is read, the nearest coherent masculine antecedent remains the sinner;
- James’s exhortation concerns the blessing of restoring the wanderer, not earning the rescuer’s own justification through another person’s repentance;
- NET accordingly translates the referent as “that person’s soul.”

Cambridge’s older Greek-oriented commentary likewise reads `ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ` as the erring brother’s soul/person and compares 1:21.

Public-domain control:
https://www.biblehub.com/commentaries/cambridge/james/5.htm

### Ezekiel-watchman alternative

A serious alternative points to Ezekiel 3/33, where a faithful warner “saves/delivers his own soul.”

Dan McCartney considers this background but argues that James places the promised result on the **actual turning of the wanderer**, whereas in Ezekiel the prophet discharges his responsibility whether or not the hearer repents.

Working locator:
- Dan G. McCartney, *James*, BECNT, discussion around pp. 263–264 / corresponding Kindle locs.

Exact original-print custody remains `AMBER` pending direct page verification.

# 7. What does `ψυχή` mean here?

Do not solve the passage through English anthropology.

`ψυχή` can denote:

- life;
- person/self;
- the living individual;
- in some contexts the inner life/soul.

Thus the phrase can legitimately be rendered conceptually as:

> save the person/life from death.

### Critical rule

The eschatological argument does **not** rest on claiming:

> `ψυχή` lexically means “immortal soul.”

It rests primarily on:

- James’s own save/death discourse;
- the future/judgment horizon of the letter;
- 1:15, 1:21, 4:12 and the Lord’s coming in ch. 5.

This is important because the physical-death reading is right to warn against overloading `ψυχή` with later body/soul dualism.

# 8. Main question: what death?

Three serious models:

1. **physical/temporal death** under divine discipline;
2. **final/eschatological death** under judgment;
3. a broad biblical death-complex in which physical judgment can be present but the statement climaxes in eschatological ruin.

The present project weights **#2 as primary**, while allowing #3 as a possible nuance.

---

# 9. Internal James control #1 — James 1:15

James opens the letter with a trajectory:

`desire → sin → sin fully grown → death`.

This is the closest internal verbal/theological control for 5:20.

Thus the letter opens with:

> sin brought to maturity produces death,

and closes with:

> turning the sinner from the wandering way saves from death.

This bookend is stronger evidence than importing a definition of death from one distant passage.

### Does 1:15 necessarily mean eternal death?

Not by lexeme alone.

Wisdom literature can speak of sin shortening life physically.

But James’s larger saving/judgment vocabulary prevents us from limiting the concept confidently to premature bodily death.

---

# 10. Internal James control #2 — James 1:21

James tells readers who in 1:18 have been brought forth by God’s word:

> receive the implanted word, which is able `σῶσαι τὰς ψυχὰς ὑμῶν` — to save your souls/lives.

This is crucial.

The same letter can address people as recipients of God’s regenerating action and still speak prospectively of salvation.

Kevin McFadden (Detroit Baptist Theological Seminary) argues precisely this:

- James assumes past regeneration/salvation in 1:18;
- yet directs readers toward final salvation in 1:21, 4:12 and 5:20;
- saving faith perseveres as living, working faith.

Source:
https://dbts.edu/2023/09/09/the-nature-of-saving-faith-in-james/

### Reformed significance

This fits a perseverance-through-means model:

> final salvation is certain in God’s saving purpose, yet the Word, warning, endurance, prayer and mutual restoration are real appointed means on the path.

It does not require:

> true justification can be possessed, lost, then re-earned by works.

---

# 11. Internal James control #3 — James 4:12

James says there is one Lawgiver and Judge:

> the One able to **save and destroy**.

The judicial context strongly supports reading James’s salvation vocabulary with an eschatological horizon.

This makes an exclusively temporal meaning in 5:20 less likely.

---

# 12. Immediate-context objection — James 5:14–16 can involve sickness and physical healing

The strongest argument for physical death is not merely lexical.

Immediately before 5:19–20 James discusses:

- sickness;
- elders;
- prayer;
- “save the sick”;
- raising up;
- sins and forgiveness;
- confession;
- healing.

Therefore a physical-life dimension in `σῴζω / θάνατος` cannot simply be mocked or dismissed.

### But the transition is not identity

5:19–20 broadens from a sick person to:

- wandering from **truth**;
- sinner’s **way**;
- death;
- multitude of sins.

The close context gives physical-death reading plausibility; the whole-letter context gives final-death reading greater weight.

Peter Davids’ influential conclusion is therefore appropriately nuanced: physical death may be included, but the tone goes beyond it toward death as an eschatological reality, especially in light of 1:15.

Locator:
- Peter H. Davids, *The Epistle of James*, NIGTC, pp. 198–201, especially p. 200 in commonly cited pagination.

Original-print quotation custody: `AMBER` until directly inspected.

# 14. MacArthur — eternal death, professing believer rather than proven regenerate apostate

MacArthur Study Bible takes:

- `among you` as a professing person within the church;
- wandering as apostasy from the faith professed;
- `sinner` in v.20 as an unregenerate/dead-faith person;
- `death` as **eternal death**, not physical death.

Accessible mirror of Study Bible note:
https://www.uecf.net/books/macarthur-study-bible-esv/chapter1341.html

### Value

This is an important conservative/Reformed guardrail:

> the seriousness of eternal death need not imply that James has narrated a regenerate man losing justification.

### Limitation

“`sinner` = necessarily unregenerate” is a theological/contextual judgment, not something the noun `ἁμαρτωλός` proves by itself.

It should not be smuggled into the lexeme.

# 17. Strong competing reading — physical death / temporal discipline

The strongest case should be represented positively.

## 17.1. Zane Hodges / Free Grace model

Hodges argues:

- `σῶσαι ψυχήν` frequently means preserve/save a **life**;
- wisdom literature often connects righteousness with prolonged life and sin with death;
- James 1:15 therefore refers to sin culminating in physical death;
- 1:21 and 5:20 likewise concern preservation from temporal death;
- 5:19 addresses brethren and a recoverable wandering believer, which fits temporal divine discipline.

Accessible primary exposition:
https://zanehodges.org/what-is-a-dead-faith-english/

## 17.2. Supporting temporal-death expositions

Grant Richison explicitly reads 5:20 as a believer spared physical death under severe discipline, appealing to 1 Cor. 11:30–32 and 1 John 5:16–17:
https://versebyversecommentary.com/2001/02/01/james-520/

The New Defender’s Study Bible likewise allows the immediate context of sickness/chastisement to point primarily toward physical death, while also warning that a merely professing Christian may face eternal death:
https://www.icr.org/bible/James/5/20/

### Strength of this model

- takes `ψυχή` as life/person seriously;
- uses wisdom background;
- respects immediate sickness/discipline context;
- avoids reading a regenerate believer as losing justification.

### Weaknesses

1. It must read **all or most** James salvation language temporally in a way that is disputed.
2. James 4:12’s Judge who can save/destroy pushes toward final judgment.
3. 1:21 follows divine begetting (1:18) yet still speaks of saving souls, which fits final-salvation/perseverance language naturally.
4. James 5 is already explicitly eschatological in 5:7–9 (coming of the Lord, Judge at the door).
5. The future `σώσει` and broad death/forgiveness formulation fit ultimate outcome well.

### Adjudication

Physical death remains `SERIOUS ALTERNATIVE`, not a straw man.

Current weighting:

- exclusively physical death: `LESS LIKELY`;
- primarily eschatological/final death: `PREFERRED`;
- eschatological death with possible physical-judgment dimension: `PLAUSIBLE NUANCED FORM`.

# 18. “Cover a multitude of sins” — what does covering mean?

The phrase resonates with biblical covering/forgiveness language.

Relevant controls:

- Ps. 32:1 — forgiven transgression / covered sin;
- Ps. 85:2 — forgiveness and covering sin;
- Prov. 10:12 — love covers offenses;
- 1 Pet. 4:8 — love covers a multitude of sins.

### Most likely here

Because 5:20 pairs:

- saving the wanderer from death;
- covering many sins,

the most coherent reading is that restoration leads to repentance/forgiveness such that the wanderer’s many sins are no longer held to condemnation.

This does **not** mean:

> the human restorer atones for sin.

Humans save/turn instrumentally in NT language (cf. Rom. 11:14; 1 Cor. 9:22; 1 Tim. 4:16), while God/Christ remains the efficient Savior.

John Gill, in Calvinistic Baptist exposition, explicitly makes this instrumental distinction and reads death as the second/eternal death while grounding covering in Christ’s blood/righteousness.

Public text:
https://biblehub.com/commentaries/james/5-20.htm

# 19. Thomas Manton — Puritan practical layer

Manton’s James exposition is particularly valuable because he takes the pastoral command seriously rather than turning 5:19–20 into an abstract security debate.

His practical observations include:

- brethren may err;
- Christians are responsible not only for their own spiritual welfare but for one another;
- Hebrews 3:12 and 12:15–16 support mutual watchfulness;
- neglect of Christian mutual care leaves wandering unchecked;
- errors are spiritually deadly;
- justification/pardon is described as God covering sin;
- a multitude of sins does not exhaust the sufficiency of free grace in Christ.

# 20. Theological guardrail — restoration text ≠ automatic loss-of-salvation text

It is tempting to reason:

1. James says “brother / among you.”
2. He wanders.
3. Someone returns him.
4. Therefore he was regenerate, ceased to be regenerate, and was regenerated again.

That conclusion does not follow from the grammar.

The text establishes:

- real community relation;
- real departure from truth;
- real mortal danger;
- real possibility/command of restoration.

It does **not** narrate:

- prior justification status;
- loss of election;
- severance and re-creation of regeneration;
- re-justification after losing justification.

### Competing conditional-security reading

A Wesleyan/Arminian interpreter may reasonably argue that the plain ecclesial address plus saving-from-death language describes a genuine believer whose eternal life becomes endangered through apostasy.

That reading must be represented fairly.

But it adds a systematic identification:

`among you / brother / wandered from truth` = `previously regenerate and justified`.

The Reformed reading denies that this identification is textually necessary while affirming every warning term at full force.

---

# 21. Opposite guardrail — perseverance doctrine must not cancel rescue

The other error is equally serious:

> “If the elect cannot finally perish, there is no urgency in restoring a wanderer.”

James refutes that pastoral inference.

He says the returner **saves from death** instrumentally.

The Reformed synthesis must therefore be:

> God preserves His people **through means**, including the Word, warnings, prayer, church discipline, mutual exhortation and active restoration.

Compare:

- Luke 22:32 — Christ’s intercession and Peter’s later strengthening of brothers;
- Gal. 6:1 — restore the overtaken person gently;
- Heb. 3:12–13 — exhort one another daily lest hardened by sin;
- Jude 22–23 — mercy/rescue/snatching from fire.

The certainty of God’s purpose gives confidence to restoration; it does not abolish restoration.

---

# 22. Relation to James 2:14–26

MacArthur’s reading uses ch.2 strongly:

- dead profession can exist;
- some inside the visible Christian sphere may have a faith-claim without saving faith;
- 5:19–20 may describe such a professor being brought to actual salvation.

That is a coherent possibility.

But 5:19–20 itself is broader than:

> “this person definitely never was regenerate.”

James intentionally gives pastoral instructions based on observable reality:

- person is among you;
- has wandered;
- is on a deathward path;
- may be restored.

The church ordinarily does not possess God’s decretal ledger before acting.

It acts on revealed duty: **pursue restoration**.

# 23. Pastoral implications

## 23.1. Do not classify the living too early

If Scripture says “bring him back,” a pastor should not rush to pronounce:

> “final apostate, no hope.”

## 23.2. Error is not merely intellectual

The language moves from:

- truth;
- to path;
- to sinner;
- to death;
- to sins.

Doctrine and life interlock.

## 23.3. Restoration is church responsibility

Manton’s emphasis is correct: members watch over one another, not as intrusive policemen but as people who believe eternal realities matter.

## 23.4. Restoration must not become works-righteousness

The restorer is an **instrument**.

God forgives; Christ atones; Spirit gives repentance/life.

## 23.5. Great sin does not make restoration pointless

“Multitude of sins” is deliberately hope-giving as well as severe.

The amount of sin does not exhaust Christ’s sufficiency.
