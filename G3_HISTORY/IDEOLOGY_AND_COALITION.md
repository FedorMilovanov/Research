# G3 Ministries — ideology, coalition, and boundary reconstruction

**Status:** ACTIVE / PUBLICATION_HOLD

## Core finding

G3’s ideological development should not be described as a simple linear move `rightward`. The evidence is better modeled as **progressive boundary-setting around a distinct conservative-Reformed Baptist identity**.

## 2013–2017 — broad conservative-Reformed coalition

Early conference rosters included a relatively broad conservative-Reformed evangelical circle: Paul Washer, Voddie Baucham, Joel Beeke, Tim Challies, Steven Lawson, James White, Conrad Mbewe, and at various points figures connected to the wider New Calvinist ecosystem.

Tim Challies, who spoke during the first eight years, later described these years warmly and remembered a collegial atmosphere with a somewhat wider circle of speakers and participants.

## 2018–2021 — social justice / CRT becomes a significant boundary

The June 2018 Dallas meeting and September 2018 `Statement on Social Justice and the Gospel` were important inflection points. The statement’s authors framed the issue as doctrinal and biblical rather than racial indifference; they opposed frameworks they believed undermined Scripture’s sufficiency and biblical anthropology.

G3’s own later writing makes clear that this concern affected practical platform/fellowship decisions. Josh Buice publicly explained that G3 had declined ministries for exhibit space and stopped inviting some previous speakers where leaders believed serious doctrinal or social-justice fault lines had emerged.

Tim Challies’ first-person retrospective adds a crucial internal perspective: before the 2021 conference he says Buice told him stronger alignment, including signing the Dallas Statement, was required for continued participation. Challies declined. This is evidence of **coalition narrowing**, not proof that the boundary itself was theologically wrong.

## 2021–2022 — a self-conscious conservative institutional ecosystem

The Church Network required close confessional alignment, historically including the Second London Baptist Confession of 1689 and the Dallas Statement (with some exceptions). G3 literature, publishing and podcasts increasingly addressed:

- CRT / social justice;
- gender and sexuality;
- complementarianism;
- abortion;
- education;
- COVID/state authority;
- Southern Baptist Convention disputes;
- worship and cultural conservatism;
- biblical counseling / sufficiency;
- political theology.

This represents an expansion beyond a conference while retaining the theological core.

## 2023 — boundary conflict moves to the right

This is essential to an objective reconstruction.

Scott Aniol published direct critiques of Christian Nationalism and `Mere Christendom`, arguing that the New Testament does not prescribe building a formally Christian nation or civil order patterned after Old Testament Israel. G3 hosted/advertised a pre-conference program containing lectures and a panel on Christian Nationalism, theonomy, postmillennialism, church/state authority and civil obedience.

As a result, G3 drew intense criticism from its own right flank. Polemical conservative outlets accused G3 of attacking Christian Nationalists, using James Lindsay-style rhetoric, and abandoning former allies.

Michael O’Fallon left the G3 board in June 2023 during this controversy. Reliable evidence supports the timing of his departure and his own public claim that he was resigning from several organizations amid conflict. The precise internal board cause remains unverified.

## Why `G3 became Christian Nationalist` is inaccurate

Primary G3/Scott Aniol material in 2023 explicitly argues **against** Christian Nationalism as a New Testament prescription. Therefore any article claiming G3 simply evolved into a Christian-nationalist institution would be factually misleading.

## Why `G3 was apolitical` is also inaccurate

G3’s broader media output included political and culture-war commentary very early, and after 2018 such issues became increasingly central to its identity and separation decisions. The conference platform itself remained substantially theological, but the ministry ecosystem was not politically neutral.

## Better model

1. **Broad conservative-Reformed conference**
2. **Anti-pragmatism / local-church / sufficiency identity**
3. **Social-justice/CRT as stronger fellowship boundary**
4. **Institutional expansion around that identity**
5. **Later refusal to follow Christian-nationalist/theonomic right flank**
6. **Increasingly distinctive middle position within conservative evangelical conflict**

## Publication guardrails

Do not write:

- `G3 was founded as a political project.`
- `G3 became a Christian Nationalist organization.`
- `Anyone who rejected the Dallas Statement was treated as a heretic.`
- `G3’s opposition to CRT proves racial indifference.`
- `G3’s opposition to Christian Nationalism proves political liberalization.`

Potentially supportable wording:

> G3’s coalition narrowed after 2018 as social-justice/CRT questions became important practical boundaries for partnership and platforming. Yet by 2023 the ministry was also drawing fire from its right flank because Scott Aniol and other G3 voices rejected Christian Nationalism and theonomy as the New Testament model for Christian political faithfulness.

This framing best fits the current primary and participant evidence.
