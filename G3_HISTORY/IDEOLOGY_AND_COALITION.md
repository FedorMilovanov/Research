# G3 Ministries — ideology, coalition, and boundary reconstruction

**Status:** ACTIVE / PUBLICATION_HOLD

## Core finding

G3’s ideological development should not be described as a simple linear move `rightward`. The evidence is better modeled as **progressive boundary-setting around a distinct conservative-Reformed Baptist identity**, with meaningful conflict first toward the evangelical center/left on social-justice questions and later toward G3’s own right flank on Christian Nationalism/theonomy.

This is not a claim that G3 occupied a politically neutral center. It is a claim about historical shape: its boundaries moved and multiplied rather than simply advancing in one ideological direction.

## 2011–2017 — theological conference before later coalition battles

The official 2011 announcement frames the future G3 Conference around God, gospel, sound doctrine and God’s glory for pastors, evangelists, missionaries and laypeople. The early national-conference themes are likewise overwhelmingly theological/ecclesial:

- 2013 — `The Gospel: Message and Mission`;
- 2014 — `The Doctrine of the Church`;
- 2015 — `The Bible is the Word of God`;
- 2016 — Trinity;
- 2017 — `Reformation 500`.

Early rosters included a relatively broad conservative-Reformed evangelical circle: Paul Washer, Voddie Baucham, Joel Beeke, Tim Challies, Steven Lawson, James White, Conrad Mbewe and figures connected to the wider New Calvinist ecosystem.

Tim Challies is particularly useful because he is a first-person participant rather than a later polemical interpreter. In his 2025 retrospective he says he spoke at the **2013 debut event** and through the first eight years, and remembers the first events as hosted in/by Pray’s Mill Baptist Church.

**Historical guardrail:** the later culture-war conflicts should not be projected backward as the founding purpose of the 2011–2017 conference.

## Political/cultural material nevertheless existed early

The conclusion above must not be turned into `early G3 was apolitical`. G3’s broader media output carried cultural/political commentary well before 2018. The distinction that matters is:

- **conference theme/program** — predominantly theology, church, Scripture, Trinity, Reformation, mission, worship;
- **broader G3 media ecosystem** — included political/cultural polemic.

The future article should quantify this difference through a dated content sample rather than using impressionistic labels.

## 2018–2021 — social justice / CRT becomes a practical fellowship boundary

The June 2018 Dallas meeting and September 2018 `Statement on Social Justice and the Gospel` were important inflection points. The statement’s signers included Josh Buice, Tom Buck, Tom Ascol, Voddie Baucham, John MacArthur, Phil Johnson, James White, Michael O’Fallon and others later important in the G3 network.

The statement’s own framing presents the dispute as doctrinal/biblical: sufficiency of Scripture, anthropology, sin, salvation, race/ethnicity, complementarianism and the church. Its existence does not itself prove that every application made in its name was sound.

G3’s own later writing makes clear that social-justice alignment affected practical partnership/platform decisions. The strongest participant evidence now comes from Tim Challies’ first-person retrospective: before the 2021 conference, he says Josh Buice told him he was no longer sufficiently aligned with G3 and that reconsideration would require signing the Dallas Statement and ceasing to promote/quote certain Christians. Challies declined.

This establishes **coalition narrowing as an experienced institutional fact**. It does not prove that signing the statement was formally required of every speaker, exhibitor or network church, nor does it by itself adjudicate whether G3’s boundary was theologically right or wrong.

## 2021–2022 — a self-conscious conservative institutional ecosystem

The Church Network required close confessional alignment, historically including the Second London Baptist Confession of 1689 and the Dallas Statement with specified requirements/exceptions. G3 simultaneously expanded beyond the annual conference into publishing, G3+, a theological journal, workshops and a church network.

Its media and program ecosystem increasingly addressed:

- CRT / social justice;
- gender and sexuality;
- complementarianism;
- abortion;
- education;
- COVID/state authority;
- Southern Baptist Convention disputes;
- worship and cultural conservatism;
- biblical counseling / sufficiency;
- political theology.

This represents institutional expansion **around** a more sharply bounded theological identity, not abandonment of the theological core.

## 2023 — the right-flank conflict is documented by G3 itself

This section is now much stronger because the key proposition no longer depends on hostile commentary.

### April 26, 2023 — `The Mixed Blessings of a Christian Nation`

Scott Aniol’s official G3 essay argues that although a Christian nation could have positive results, its negative consequences would outweigh the positives, especially with respect to the church’s central mission.

This is a direct institutional publication and should control over secondary descriptions of what Aniol supposedly believed.

### April 27, 2023 — internal complexity, not a monolithic script

Virgil Walker’s official G3 essay `The Dangerous Intersection of Christian Nationalism and Ethnocentrism` begins from a more qualified posture: if Christian Nationalism merely means active patriotism, borders, sovereignty and return toward a Judeo-Christian ethic, he says one could call him a Christian nationalist, before warning about ethnocentric/nationalist forms.

This matters because `G3 opposed Christian Nationalism` can itself be too compressed. Different G3 voices emphasized different definitions and dangers.

### May 3, 2023 — `Christian Faithfulness: The Biblical Alternative to Christian Nationalism`

Aniol states directly that one of his biggest objections is that **the New Testament does not present anything like Christian Nationalism** as the Christian model. His proposed alternative is `Christian Faithfulness`: Christians as citizens of a heavenly kingdom who nevertheless care for this world.

This directly disproves any later claim that G3’s official 2023 line was simply to endorse Christian Nationalism.

### G3 review of `Mere Christendom`

Aniol’s review of Doug Wilson’s *Mere Christendom*, also published through G3 / *Gloria Deo Journal of Theology* Volume 2 (2023), says that he disagreed theologically with Stephen Wolfe’s proposal and treats Wilson’s Christendom project critically from a Baptist/non-postmillennial perspective.

The journal object is especially valuable because it is an institutional publication rather than an ephemeral social-media exchange.

### Official 2023 pre-conference program

G3’s own schedule placed political theology at the center of a dedicated pre-conference day:

- James White — non-compliance / civil authority questions;
- Owen Strachan — evaluating Christian Nationalism;
- Scott Aniol — higher kingdom / church and worship;
- panel explicitly described as a friendly debate over **Christian Nationalism, theonomy and postmillennialism**.

Therefore two claims can be true at once:

1. political/culture questions had become much more explicit in G3 programming;
2. G3 was not simply adopting the Christian-nationalist/theonomic position—it was debating and, in Aniol’s case, rejecting it as the New Testament prescription.

## Michael O’Fallon’s June 2023 departure — stronger chronology, causal HOLD

A contemporaneous report initially noticed O’Fallon’s removal from the online G3 board list. After publication, it reproduced a public statement by O’Fallon in which he said that the previous week he had **submitted his own resignation** from G3, the Conservative Baptist Network and Alpha and Omega Ministries.

A separate Baptist News Global report on June 27, 2023 reproduced the same participant statement: O’Fallon said he resigned to prevent ministries he served from continually having to answer accusations attached to his name and Sovereign Nations work.

This substantially strengthens the narrow facts:

- he held a G3 board position before departure;
- by late June 2023 he had left it;
- he publicly characterized the departure as his own resignation;
- the departure occurred amid a highly polarized political-theology controversy.

It does **not** establish:

- that G3 formally removed him;
- that disagreement over Christian Nationalism was the sole or direct board cause;
- that G3 demanded his resignation;
- that O’Fallon’s public explanation discloses every internal factor.

**Article wording:** say he resigned during the controversy and give his stated reason; keep exact internal causation on HOLD.

## Criticism from the right is evidence of reception, not of truth

Polemical conservative outlets in 2023 accused G3 of attacking Christian Nationalists, borrowing James Lindsay-style categories, abandoning allies or becoming `TG3`/too close to the evangelical establishment.

Those outlets are useful for documenting **how G3 was received by a right-flank constituency**. They are not needed to prove G3’s substantive position, because G3’s own essays and conference program now establish that position directly.

This distinction prevents circular research:

> criticism that `G3 opposed Christian Nationalism` is corroborative reception evidence; Aniol/G3 primary texts are the evidence of what G3 actually argued.

## Why `G3 became Christian Nationalist` is inaccurate

Primary G3/Scott Aniol material in April–May 2023 explicitly argues against Christian Nationalism as a New Testament prescription. The 2023 *Gloria Deo* review further criticizes Christian-nationalist/Christendom proposals, and the official pre-conference presents the issue as a debate rather than settled G3 adoption.

Therefore any article claiming G3 simply evolved into a Christian-nationalist institution would be factually misleading.

## Why `G3 moved left in 2023` is also too simple

Opposition to Christian Nationalism did not mean political liberalization. G3 remained strongly conservative on sexuality, gender, abortion, biblical authority, complementarianism, CRT/social justice and church/state questions. Aniol’s alternative was explicitly conservative Baptist political theology, not progressive evangelical politics.

The better historical description is **boundary conflict on two flanks**.

## Better model

1. **2011–2017 — broad conservative-Reformed theological conference**
2. **persistent anti-pragmatism / local-church / sufficiency identity**
3. **2018–2021 — social-justice/CRT becomes a stronger fellowship boundary**
4. **2021–2022 — institutional ecosystem expansion around that identity**
5. **2023 — political theology becomes explicit, while G3 refuses the Christian-nationalist/theonomic right flank**
6. **2023 onward — increasingly distinctive position inside conservative evangelical conflict rather than a one-axis left/right progression**

## Quantification still required

The qualitative model is now strong, but the article should eventually quantify it.

Build a dated sample of G3 publications/sessions for each year or defined period and code at least:

- gospel / Christ / salvation;
- church / ecclesiology;
- Scripture / sufficiency;
- missions;
- worship;
- social justice / CRT / race;
- gender / sexuality / complementarianism;
- abortion / family;
- state / civil authority / COVID;
- Christian Nationalism / theonomy / postmillennialism;
- SBC / evangelical-institution disputes;
- education / cultural formation.

Only after that should the final article use quantitative language such as `increasingly central`, `dominant`, `marginal` or `rare`.

## Publication guardrails

Do not write:

- `G3 was founded as a political project.`
- `G3 became a Christian Nationalist organization.`
- `G3 became politically liberal in 2023.`
- `Anyone who rejected the Dallas Statement was treated as a heretic.`
- `Every G3 leader held the same definition of Christian Nationalism.`
- `G3’s opposition to CRT proves racial indifference.`
- `O’Fallon was fired for supporting Christian Nationalism.`
- `O’Fallon’s resignation proves there was no internal disagreement.`

Potentially supportable wording:

> G3’s coalition narrowed after 2018 as social-justice and CRT questions became important practical boundaries for partnership and platforming. Yet that narrowing did not produce a simple march toward the political right. In 2023 Scott Aniol used G3’s own platform to reject Christian Nationalism as the New Testament prescription and to critique modern Christendom projects, while G3 devoted a pre-conference program to debating Christian Nationalism, theonomy and postmillennialism. The result was a new conflict with part of the ministry’s own right flank. Michael O’Fallon resigned from G3’s board during that controversy, although the evidence does not establish political theology as the sole internal cause of his departure.

This framing best fits the current primary and participant evidence.
