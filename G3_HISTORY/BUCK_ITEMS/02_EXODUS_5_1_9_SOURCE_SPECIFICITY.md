## 3. Rows must be separated by specificity

### A. Low-to-medium specificity / text-driven or ordinary theological material

Several alleged parallels are real-looking textual similarities in the dossier but are not independently strong enough to bear a major plagiarism conclusion by themselves:

- Moses/Aaron approaching the world's most powerful ruler;
- Pharaoh refusing and the brothers repeating God's demand;
- Pharaoh having been warned;
- unbelief/rebellion as spiritual rather than merely intellectual;
- sinners being commanded to repent and believe;
- warning of judgment for rejecting Christ.

These may still show dependence **in cumulative context**, but the propositions are ordinary consequences of Exodus 5 and standard evangelical application. They should not receive an `A` grade merely because wording overlaps.

**Working class:** `C → possible B cumulatively`.

### B. Consecutive narrative sequence around Moses' preparation

The dossier places several close sentences in sequence around ~10:20–10:37:

- Moses had seen God's glory in the burning bush;
- heard the Great I Am announce salvation;
- initially had trouble believing;
- then trusted God's promises and obeyed God's commands;
- God enabled him to do what he was called to do.

Individual clauses can arise naturally from the text. The **multi-sentence sequence and close verbal ordering**, if the original audio verifies it, is more probative than any one clause.

**Working class:** `U → potential B`, contingent on audio, exact Ryken text and attribution context.

### C. Louvre / Rameses II worship-release evidence

The dossier says both source and sermon invoke ancient Egyptian evidence that slaves could receive time away to worship. This is a secondary historical datum rather than a distinctive theological inference.

The relevant question is not merely whether Buck used the same fact but whether he reproduced Ryken's selection, framing or wording without attribution and whether the fact was independently common in reference works.

**Working class:** `U / source-lineage check required`.

### D. High-specificity candidate — `Keep making those bricks, you loafers...`

The dossier presents the closest-looking creative-language candidate at ~27:19:

- Ryken side: an imagined command beginning `Keep making those bricks, you loafers!` and continuing with finding one's own straw;
- Buck side: substantially the same creative sentence.

The dossier itself correctly distinguishes this as a **creative paraphrase rather than a Bible quotation**. A web exact-phrase sweep in the current pass did not surface an independent earlier source for the full formulation. Absence from search is not proof of originality, but it increases the need to test the exact Ryken object rather than downgrade the sentence as generic Exodus language.

If the original Buck audio reproduces the full distinctive formulation without attribution and the exact Ryken edition confirms it, this is a serious `B` candidate and could approach `A` depending on context/extent.

**Current class:** `U → potential B/A`.

### E. `two choices: courageous faith or rebellious unbelief`

The dossier shows a sustained closing formulation on both sides around ~31:27, including the contrast between Moses and Pharaoh. The theological binary itself is not unique, but the full phrasing and sequence are more specific than the underlying idea.

**Working class:** `U → potential B` if audio/source object verify close verbal identity.

## 4. Why the dossier's binary labels are insufficient

The item contains at least four different evidence types:

1. text-driven narrative/exegesis;
2. standard evangelical application;
3. secondary historical evidence;
4. creative/non-biblical formulation.

Treating all of these as equivalent `plagiarism instances` would inflate the evidentiary count. Conversely, recognizing that some rows are low-specificity must not be used to erase a genuinely distinctive creative-language match.
